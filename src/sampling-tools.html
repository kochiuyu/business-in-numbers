Excel + Python sampling desk

Five complete, independent scripts reproduce the teaching examples below. All data are synthetic. Use Python 3.10+ with NumPy, SciPy, and matplotlib: python -m pip install numpy scipy matplotlib. Checked with NumPy 2.3.5, SciPy 1.17.0, and matplotlib 3.10.8. Copy one complete script into the indicated filename and run it. No downloads, credentials, or live business data are required. Simulation uses fixed random seeds; tiny numerical differences across software versions are possible.

Normal functions take standard deviation, not variance. The variance of the mean is σ²/n; its standard error is σ/√n. For one observed sample, use sample SD s and report s/√n as an estimated SE. Increasing the number R of simulated samples improves the Monte Carlo estimate; increasing n changes the sampling distribution itself.

1 · Enumerate all service-credit samples, n = 1 through 5

Each independent draw gives a $10, $20, or $30 service credit with probability 1/3. Sampling is with replacement. For n=2 there are nine ordered samples but only five distinct means: 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, with counts 1, 2, 3, 2, 1. Equal probability for ordered samples does not mean equal probability for distinct means.

In Excel enter 10, 20, 30 in A2:A4. =AVERAGE(A2:A4) gives μ=20, =VAR.P(A2:A4) gives σ²=66.666667, and =STDEV.P(A2:A4) gives σ=8.164966. These three entries describe the entire equally weighted population, so use population functions here.

For n=2 enter the nine ordered pairs in D2:E10: (10,10), (10,20), (10,30), (20,10), (20,20), (20,30), (30,10), (30,20), (30,30). In F2 enter =AVERAGE(D2:E2) and fill down. Put the five means in H2:H6; use =COUNTIF($F$2:$F$10,H2)/9 in I2 and fill down. Check =SUM(I2:I6) equals 1; =SUMPRODUCT(H2:H6,I2:I6) equals 20; =SUMPRODUCT(I2:I6,(H2:H6-20)^2) equals 33.333333. Plot H:I as separated columns labeled “Sample mean ($)” and “Probability.”

Run python enumeration.py. It prints the complete law for every n, including 81 ordered samples at n=4 and 243 at n=5. The assertions verify all probability sums, means, and variances. This with-replacement model does not apply unchanged to a finite sample without replacement; those draws are dependent.

2 · See LLN and CLT without confusing n and R

Support duration has an exponential population distribution with mean 12 minutes and SD 12 minutes. One growing sample illustrates the law of large numbers (LLN). A different experiment takes R=30,000 independent samples at each fixed n=1, 5, 30, 100. Each row of the Python matrix is one sample; averaging across columns produces R sample means.

LLN says the probability of being far from μ becomes small as n grows. It does not promise a path improves at every step. The central limit theorem (CLT) describes the approximately normal shape of the standardized mean when n is sufficiently large under suitable assumptions. Here the exact mean law is Gamma(shape n, scale 12/n). The raw population stays skewed even when sample means become nearly normal. No fixed cutoff such as n=30 or 35 guarantees a good tail approximation.

Excel experiment: in A2 enter =-12*LN(1-RAND()) and fill down to A10001. In B2 enter =AVERAGE($A$2:A2) and fill down for the running mean. Make an XY scatter chart of observation number against column B and add a horizontal line at 12. To study a fixed n=30, fill A2:AD1001 with independent exponential draws, then use =AVERAGE(A2:AD2) in AE2 and fill down. This produces R=1,000 sample means, each based on n=30 observations. Compare =STDEV.S(AE2:AE1001) to =12/SQRT(30). RAND recalculates and is not seedable in this formula workflow; copy and paste as values to freeze a run.

Run python simulation.py --output-dir sampling-plots. The script writes lln.svg and clt.svg and prints the checked output below. Histogram heights are densities. The displayed Z range is finite and omits some tail observations; histogram weights preserve the total-R normalization instead of renormalizing the visible range.

The delivery calculation is exact if the observations are independent normal variables; with only a stated mean and SD, it is a CLT approximation whose adequacy depends on the population. For conversions, nπ=24 and n(1−π)=56 comfortably exceed common screening rules of 5 or 10. These checks are not an error guarantee. “Fewer than 20” means K≤19, not K≤20. The latter event has probability 0.197846577. Half a count makes a substantial difference here: the continuity correction reduces absolute approximation error from 0.029334 to 0.000903. Use the exact binomial when software is available.

4 · Use sample SD and the correct t reference

An independent normal sample of n=16 observations has observed mean 47 and sample SD 8. Against μ₀=50, estimated SE=8/√16=2 and observed T=(47−50)/2=−1.5, with 15 degrees of freedom. Under that null model, the left-tail p-value is 0.077183330 and the two-sided p-value is 0.154366660. The t law describes the ratio with a random denominator across repeated samples. Holding the realized s=8 fixed does not turn this into a predictive probability for a future unstandardized mean.

Read a t table: first locate df=n−1, then identify whether its heading is one-tail area, two-tail area, or central coverage. A central 95% interval leaves 2.5% in each tail. If a printed table lacks your df, using the next smaller df gives a larger conventional two-sided cutoff, but is not an exact probability calculation. Software can use the exact df. At df=15, the positive upper-tail 10% and 5% cutoffs are 1.340606 and 1.753050, so T=−1.5 has a left-tail area between .05 and .10.

5 · Inspect normality before relying on a small-sample t law

Start with the sampling design, a histogram or boxplot, and a normal Q–Q plot. In a Q–Q plot, ordered observations are plotted against theoretical normal quantiles. Rough linearity is compatible with normality; systematic bends suggest skewness or tail differences. A few points in a sample of 16 provide limited information. Neither a straight-looking plot nor a large test p-value proves normality or independence.

Excel Q–Q workflow: for 16 numeric observations in A2:A17, put ranks 1 through 16 in C2:C17. In D2 use =SMALL($A$2:$A$17,C2); in E2 use =NORM.S.INV((C2-0.5)/COUNT($A$2:$A$17)); fill both down. Make an XY scatter chart with E on the horizontal axis and D on the vertical axis. These plotting positions differ slightly from SciPy's default probability plot positions, so endpoints need not be identical. Investigate outliers in context rather than deleting them to make a plot straight.

Moment conventions: a normal population has skewness 0, raw kurtosis 3, and excess kurtosis 0. Excel =KURT(A2:A17) returns an adjusted excess estimate, not raw kurtosis. Excel SKEW and KURT use finite-sample adjustments and should not be plugged into the conventional Jarque–Bera formula expecting the same output as SciPy. This script explicitly uses unadjusted central-moment skewness and kurtosis and verifies JB = n/6 × [skewness² + excess kurtosis²/4].

Jarque–Bera compares that statistic to an asymptotic chi-square reference with 2 df. Small-sample calibration can be poor and power is limited, especially at n=16; a failure to reject cannot establish that a small-sample t calculation is exact. Large samples can reveal departures too small to matter operationally. These diagnostics concern marginal shape, not dependence, selection bias, or representativeness.

Run python diagnostics.py --output-dir sampling-plots. It generates qq.svg and prints sample SD with ddof=1, estimated SE, skewness, both kurtosis conventions, and the JB result. These new synthetic draws are not the fixed-summary t case above.
