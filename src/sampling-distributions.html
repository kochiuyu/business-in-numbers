START HERE · YOUR LEARNING PATH

Can one week of tickets guide a staffing decision?

A support director cannot review every ticket. A sample average looks useful, but a different set of tickets would give a different answer. Your job in this class is to explain how much it could change before anyone treats a small improvement as a real change in the process.

Bring from Class 3: a random variable assigns a number to an uncertain outcome; its distribution describes possible values and probabilities; a mean is its center and an SD its spread. If these are unfamiliar, revisit random variables. Here the new random variable will be an average of several observations.

  1. Define what is sampled (1–2): distinguish a ticket, a sample of tickets and an average across a sample. Use the tiny service-credit model to see every possible sample.
  2. Explain what averaging changes (3–5): learn standard error, then separate the LLN’s concentration from the CLT’s approximate shape.
  3. Use that uncertainty (6–10): calculate unusual averages and conversion counts, then account for an unknown population SD.
  4. Defend the result (11–12): check the design, reproduce one calculation and complete the transfer task.

Read the numbered lessons in order. Before each lab, predict the result in words; then change one input and explain what moved. Essential explanations and answer checks do not require Technical depth, which adds derivations.

STEP 01 OF 10

Choose the population

{{samvisual:design}}

A bigger file can still answer the wrong question

A support manager needs the mean duration across all tickets. In this separate synthetic case, 80% of tickets are routine and 20% complex. Their population mean durations are 10 and 30 minutes. The target therefore counts routine tickets four times as heavily: 0.80 × 10 + 0.20 × 30 = 14 minutes.

An analyst deliberately samples equal numbers from each group to learn about complex cases. That can be a useful design, but the unweighted sample mean gives each group half the influence. Across repeated samples within the groups, it is centered at 0.50 × 10 + 0.50 × 30 = 20 minutes, not 14.

Predict: if both group sample sizes grow from 50 to 500 while the split stays equal, will the unweighted mean become a reliable estimate of the all-ticket mean?

Check the target, then choose the weights

No. Under representative independent sampling within each group, more observations stabilize the unweighted mean near 20. They do not remove its six-minute mismatch with the 14-minute target. Instead, estimate each group mean and combine them as 0.80 × routine sample mean + 0.20 × complex sample mean. The weights describe the target population, not the convenience of the exported file.

Those population shares must be known or estimated appropriately. Use an uncertainty calculation for this stratified design, not automatically the ordinary IID standard error of the pooled records. Weighting cannot repair biased sampling within a group or an entirely missing group.

Whose satisfaction rate is 80%?

In a separate synthetic survey, the target is all 100 customers served in one week. All 100 are invited; 60 reply, and 48 of those report being satisfied. Satisfaction is a yes/no outcome. The response rate is 60/100 = 60%; the satisfaction rate among respondents is 48/60 = 80%. Those denominators answer different questions.

Predict: do the records establish that 80 of the 100 customers were satisfied? Keep the 40 missing answers visible before checking.

Find what the missing answers allow

We know that 48 customers were satisfied and 12 were not. If none of the 40 nonrespondents were satisfied, the all-customer rate is 48/100 = 48%. If all 40 were satisfied, it is 88/100 = 88%. Every compatible rate lies between those endpoints, in one-percentage-point steps for these 100 customers. These are bounds from the missing answers, not a 95% confidence interval.

Try an explicit assumption: if half the nonrespondents were satisfied, there would be 48 + 20 = 68 satisfied customers, or 68%. If 80% of nonrespondents were satisfied, the total would be 48 + 32 = 80, or 80%. The observed replies alone cannot choose between those assumptions.

All 100 target customers were invited, so this example isolates nonresponse rather than random selection of invitees. A standard sampling-error calculation around 80% does not account for the unknown answers. Do not code missing answers as “not satisfied,” or assume respondents represent nonrespondents without justification.

Report and act: state both rates and their denominators, explain the missing answers, and investigate why people did not respond. A follow-up can improve coverage; weighting requires relevant information and assumptions. More precise calculations on the same respondents do not recover the absent responses. This is the population version of Class 1’s blank-versus-zero check.

01A sample is a design, not just a smaller filePopulation, observations, independence and representativeness+

First choose whose average matters

A staffing decision needs the average for the tickets the team will actually serve, not just the easiest records to export. If weekends are missing, even a very large weekday sample can answer the wrong question. Start by naming the population and how each observation enters the sample; only then does a probability model have a business meaning.

A support director wants the average duration of all eligible tickets this quarter. That collection is the population. The tickets selected for review form a sample. One ticket’s duration is an observation. The target population, period and measurement must be defined before calculating anything.

Parameter

A fixed feature of the population, usually unknown: mean μ, variance σ² or conversion proportion π.

Random sample

Before selection, X₁,…,Xₙ are random variables. After selection, x₁,…,xₙ are the observed numbers. A sample is the collection, not one value.

Inference

Use a well-designed sample to learn about a population while retaining uncertainty. More rows cannot repair a systematically unrepresentative source.

The working model: independent, identically distributed draws

In this lesson, IID means every Xᵢ follows the same population distribution and the draws are independent. Drawing with replacement from a finite population is one way to obtain this model. Each draw has the population’s distributional properties; a realized sample need not have its exact median, range, variance or shape.

{{math:iid}}

Finite-population simple random sampling is different. Without replacement, each size-n subset is equally likely, but the draws are dependent. Equal individual inclusion chances alone do not guarantee a simple random sample. This lesson explicitly labels its with-replacement examples.

Technical depth · The finite-population correction

{{math:finite-population}}

At n=N, a census has zero sampling variance. For n much smaller than N, the correction is near 1. If population variance instead uses divisor N−1, the equivalent expression is (1−n/N)S²population/n.

Business checks before formulas

  • Coverage: an app-only survey may miss customers who buy by phone.
  • Dependence: tickets from one outage or purchases within one store can move together.
  • Nonresponse: satisfied and dissatisfied customers may respond at different rates.
  • Stratification: sample separately within meaningful groups, then combine their means with the population weights. If online customers are 60% of the population and offline customers 40%, use those weights, not automatically the sample split. This can improve precision; it does not automatically fix missing groups or nonresponse.

02One population. Many possible sample means.An exact sampling distribution you can enumerate+

Now make the summary itself random

Class 3 asked, “What amount could one claim pay?” We now ask, “What average could two claims produce?” Imagine drawing two claims, recording their average on a new sheet, replacing the claims and repeating. The new sheet contains averages, not claims. Its distribution is the sampling distribution. The small credit example below makes that thought experiment exact rather than relying on a simulation.

A statistic is a function of the observed sample that does not contain unknown population parameters. The sample mean, sample variance and sample median are examples. Before data arrive, a statistic is random; after observation, it has one realized value.

{{math:sample-mean}}

For a synthetic service-credit model, each independent claim pays $10, $20 or $30 with equal probability. The population mean is $20 and its variance is 200/3 dollars². Imagine repeated draws with replacement from those three amounts.

With n=2, there are 3²=9 equally likely ordered pairs. The pairs (10,30), (20,20) and (30,10) all produce a $20 mean. So the sample means are not equally likely, even though the pairs are.

  1. Predict: for n=2, is a mean of $20 more likely than a mean of $10? List the pairs that produce each.
  2. Change: enumerate n=1, then n=2, then n=5. Keep the three possible claim amounts unchanged.
  3. Observe: compare the center and spread, then open “Every mean and its probability.”
  4. Explain: say what one bar represents. Is it a claim amount, an observed sample, or an average across possible samples?
Check your explanation

For n=2, $20 occurs in three of nine ordered pairs, while $10 occurs only in (10,10). Their probabilities are 1/3 and 1/9. The mean remains centered at $20 as n grows, while extreme averages become less likely. The original claim distribution is unchanged: we changed how many claims are averaged.

Exact calculation. No random simulation and no normal approximation.

03Same center. Less spread.Unbiasedness, variance and standard error+

Turn the picture into a measure of reliability

The exact chart becomes more concentrated around $20 as n grows. We need a number for that spread so a manager can distinguish noisy estimates from stable ones. Call it the standard error: the standard deviation of the sample mean across repeated samples. The formulas below say that averaging preserves the population center but divides variance by n; taking the square root gives SD divided by √n.

{{math:mean-properties}}

Unbiased means centered correctly across repeated sampling. It does not mean every sample average equals μ. Averaging a finite number of repeated sample means will usually be close to μ, not identical to it.

SD versus SE: population SD describes differences between individual tickets. Standard error is the SD of a statistic across samples. Increasing n does not make individual customers more alike; it makes the sample average more stable.

In the credit model, n=1,2,3 gives sample-mean variances 200/3, 100/3 and 200/9 dollars². For n=2, the weighted squared deviations are [100×1 + 25×2 + 0×3 + 25×2 + 100×1]/9 = 100/3. The explorer computes the same calculation for every n.

Technical depth · Derive the expectation

{{math:mean-proof}}

This expectation calculation needs equal means, not independence. Independence enters the variance calculation.

Technical depth · Derive the variance and locate dependence

{{math:variance-proof}}

Positive correlation can make the IID formula understate uncertainty. If all pairs have covariance ρσ², the variance is σ²[1+(n−1)ρ]/n, for a valid joint covariance structure. More correlated observations are not equivalent to more independent information.

04Why averaging settles downThe law of large numbers, without a false guarantee+

Does more information eventually help?

Standard error gives a size for sampling noise. The next question is whether that noise becomes small enough to trust an average as the sample grows. Return to support durations: one very long ticket can dominate a small sample, but it has less influence in a large one. The law of large numbers formalizes this increasing stability under the sampling assumptions; it is not a promise that the next ticket will improve today’s estimate.

Suppose independent support durations have mean 12 minutes and finite variance 144 minutes². As more tickets enter a sample, its average becomes more likely to lie close to 12. A particular running average can still move away from 12 after the next ticket.

{{math:lln}}

This is convergence in probability. It does not say that finite-sample variation disappears, that the average eventually equals μ exactly, or that every extra observation improves the estimate. It also does not repair selection bias.

Technical depth · Markov: bound the tail of a nonnegative variable

{{math:markov}}

The argument works for discrete or continuous variables with a finite expectation. For a nonnegative duration with mean 12 minutes, P(X≥60)≤12/60=.20. This bound is valid but may be loose.

05Why averages look more normalCLT shape versus LLN concentration+

Stability is not yet a probability calculation

The LLN tells the director that large samples tend to be close to the target. To ask how often an average crosses a particular threshold, we also need the distribution’s shape. The CLT supplies an approximate normal shape for the standardized error: subtract the population mean and divide by the standard error. This is the bridge from averaging to the normal probabilities you learned in Class 3.

The LLN tells us the average concentrates near μ. The central limit theorem describes the shape of its error after scaling by its standard error. These are related but different claims.

{{math:clt}}{{math:clt-approx}}

Not “everything becomes normal.” The population does not change. The unstandardized mean concentrates at μ, while the standardized mean approaches a nondegenerate bell curve. A cutoff such as n≥30 or n≥35 is a heuristic, not a theorem: severe skew, rare extremes, dependence or infinite variance can invalidate it.

Try it · Two controls, two different effects

  1. Predict: will 100 tickets per sample make individual ticket durations less skewed? Will 5,000 repetitions make each average more precise?
  2. Change: hold B=2000 and compare n=1, 5, 30 and 100. Then hold n=30 and compare B=100 with B=5000.
  3. Observe: compare the individual-ticket panel, the spread of repeated averages and the reported theoretical SE. Open the running-average view for a different perspective.
  4. Explain: which change would cost the business more observations for one estimate, and which merely gives the computer more experiments?
Check your explanation

Increasing n narrows the distribution of averages; at n=1 the SE is 12 minutes and at n=100 it is 1.2. The individual population stays skewed. Increasing B makes the simulated picture more stable, but leaves the theoretical SE at 12/√30 when n=30. A running average follows one accumulating sample; the histogram compares many separate samples.

Technical depth · A useful boundary of the theorem

The normal limit requires the stated conditions. IID Cauchy observations, for example, have no finite mean or variance; their average remains Cauchy rather than concentrating as in this demonstration. Heavy-tailed data with finite variance can still converge slowly. For an exactly normal population, the sample mean is normal at every n, so no CLT approximation is needed.

06Turn a business threshold into a z-scoreKnown population SD, sample mean probabilities+

Use the right unit of surprise

Suppose a delivery team reports an average three minutes faster than a stable 40-minute benchmark. Three minutes may be ordinary variation for one delivery yet unusual for an average of many deliveries. First identify the random quantity in the question: here it is the average of 36 deliveries. Next express its distance from the benchmark in units of SE, not individual-delivery SD. Finally read the corresponding tail probability.

A stable delivery process has population mean 40 minutes and known population SD 12 minutes. For 36 independent deliveries, what is the probability that their average is at most 37 minutes? The standard error is 12/√36=2, so the standardized cutoff is −1.5 and the probability is about .066807.

{{math:z-mean}}

Try it · Why a bigger sample changes surprise

  1. Predict: keeping the cutoff at 37, will the left-tail probability rise or fall when n increases from 36 to 144?
  2. Change: keep μ=40 and σ=12; calculate at n=36 and n=144.
  3. Observe: record the SE, z-score and shaded area at each size.
  4. Explain: why does the same three-minute gap provide different evidence?
Check your explanation

The SE falls from 2 to 1 minute. The gap is now −3 rather than −1.5 standard errors, so the left-tail probability falls from about .0668 to .00135 under this model. The business gap did not get larger; random averaging noise got smaller.

07A conversion rate is an average of zeros and onesBernoulli observations, counts and proportions+

The same reasoning also works for percentages

A support business may track the fraction of visitors who convert as well as minutes or dollars. A rate looks like a new kind of statistic, but adding a 1 for each conversion and a 0 otherwise, then dividing by the number of visitors, is just averaging. We can therefore reuse the mean and SE reasoning rather than learn an unrelated recipe. The count and the proportion describe the same outcomes in different units.

Let Xᵢ=1 when visitor i converts and 0 otherwise. Assume independent visits with the same conversion probability π. The population mean is π, and the sample mean is the observed conversion proportion p̂. Keep the unknown population parameter and the random estimate distinct.

{{math:binary-moments}}{{math:proportion-def}}{{math:proportion-moments}}

For π=.30 and n=80, E[p̂]=.30 and SE(p̂)≈.051235, or 5.12 percentage points. The count has mean 24 and SD √16.8≈4.09878 conversions. Dividing a count by n changes both the center and scale.

Technical depth · Derive the count approximation from the proportion

{{math:binary-clt}}

Multiplication by n multiplies the variance by n². At π=0 or 1 the sample is deterministic and the standardization divides by zero; use the exact result instead.

08Translate the event before using the bell curveStrict inequalities and continuity correction+

A business phrase must become an exact event

A team worries about “fewer than 20 conversions.” Before looking up a probability, list the allowed counts: 0 through 19. A smooth curve has no integer steps, so approximating that event requires a boundary between 19 and 20. This is why the continuity correction is about the event’s meaning, not an arbitrary extra .5 in a formula.

Among 80 visits with π=.30, what is the chance of fewer than 20 conversions? Since K is integer-valued, K<20 means K≤19. Equivalently, p̂<.25 means p̂≤19/80=.2375.

{{math:continuity}}

The exact binomial probability is .135223. Using the uncorrected boundary 20 gives about .164557, visibly different. A normal curve spreads probability over intervals; the half-unit adjustment aligns those intervals with integer counts.

  4. Explain: why is the difference between the two exact answers the probability of exactly 20 conversions?
Check your explanation

“Fewer than 20” uses k=19. “At most 20” also includes the entire K=20 outcome, so its probability is higher. The normal boundary for the first event is 19.5. Continuity correction helps the approximation but does not make it exact.

09Estimating the scale adds uncertaintySample variance and the Student t pivot+

The model’s spread is usually another unknown

So far we supplied the population SD as if the director knew it. In practice the same sample often estimates both the center and the spread. That introduces a second source of uncertainty: an unusually small sample SD can make an ordinary mean look extreme. Estimate variance first, then distinguish the estimated SE from the true SE before using the t distribution.

Usually population SD σ is unknown. Estimate it with the sample SD S, using divisor n−1 for sample variance. Use uppercase S before the sample is observed and lowercase s for its realized value.

{{math:sample-variance}}

S²/n estimates the variance of the sample mean; S/√n estimates its standard error. Those are different quantities. Under IID finite-variance sampling, E[S²]=σ², but S is not generally an unbiased estimate of σ.

The exact normal-sampling result

{{math:normal-sample}}{{math:t-pivot}}

The standardized statistic T follows t, not the raw sample mean. Compared with z, its denominator changes from sample to sample. The heavier tails account for uncertainty in that estimated scale. n−1 degrees of freedom arise because the n deviations from the sample mean sum to zero.

Technical depth · Build t from independent normal and chi-square variables

{{math:chi-square-sample}}{{math:t-proof}}

The chi-square variance result and mean–variance independence are special to the normal model. They are not consequences of IID sampling alone.

10Ask the right probability questionObserved t-statistics, tails and t-tables+

Connect uncertainty to a benchmark question

We can now revisit the staffing decision: is a 47-minute sample mean surprising if the process still averages 50 minutes? Subtract 50 from 47 to get the observed gap, then divide by the estimated SE of 2 minutes. The resulting −1.5 measures the gap in estimated-error units. The t reference distribution accounts for the fact that those units were estimated from only 16 observations.

A process benchmark is μ₀=50 minutes. An independent normal-model sample of n=16 tickets has observed mean x̄=47 and sample SD s=8. The estimated SE is 8/4=2, so tobs=(47−50)/2=−1.5.

{{math:t-tail}}

The left-tail calculation asks how often a studentized statistic this low would arise under the benchmark model. It previews a one-sided p-value, not the probability that μ=50 is true. Choose a one-sided or two-sided question from the business question, not after seeing which tail is smaller.

Do not turn observed s into a known σ. The t-tail above is not a predictive probability that a future sample mean will be below 47. Observing s=8 does not make σ=8. Under normality, conditioning on S=s leaves the mean’s normal distribution dependent on the unknown σ. A future-mean probability requires more modeling or a separate predictive method.

Try it · Separate the estimated gap from uncertainty

  1. Predict: with mean 47, benchmark 50 and n=16 fixed, will doubling s from 8 to 16 make the result more or less unusual?
  2. Change: calculate both SD settings, leaving all other inputs fixed.
  3. Observe: compare the estimated SE, t-statistic and left-tail probability.
  4. Explain: why does a larger tail probability not mean that the benchmark has become more likely to be true?
Check your explanation

The estimated SE doubles from 2 to 4, and t moves from −1.5 to −.75. A result at least this low is less unusual under the benchmark, so the left-tail probability rises. Both probabilities assume the benchmark model; neither assigns a probability to that model itself.

Read a two-tail t-table correctly

Rows are degrees of freedom ν, not sample sizes. For central area 1−α, the positive critical value c satisfies P(−c<Tν<c)=1−α, leaving α/2 in each tail. The software section contains a checked table spanning small, medium and large ν.

{{math:t-critical}}

For ν=15, central 90% uses c≈1.753 and central 95% uses c≈2.131. Since 1.5<1.753, the left tail below −1.5 exceeds .05. If the exact df row is absent, a smaller-df row gives a conventional conservative critical value, not an exact substitute for its CDF.

Technical depth · Large samples and what does not become exact

As ν grows, tν approaches N(0,1). Under IID nonnormal sampling with finite positive variance, consistency of S together with the CLT yields asymptotic standard normality of the studentized mean. Exact finite-sample t still requires the normal model; “large” is not a universal fixed cutoff.

STEP 09 OF 10

Check the sampling design

{{samvisual:diagnosis}}

Count stores as well as receipts

A retailer collects 25 receipts from each of four independently sampled stores: 100 rows. Receipts within a store share its local conditions. This separate teaching model gives every receipt a population SD of $12 and gives two different receipts from the same store a correlation of 0.20. Different stores are independent, stores have equal size in the target, and sampling within each store is representative.

Ignoring the shared conditions would give SE = 12/√100 = $1.20. But each receipt has 24 correlated neighbors. The variance of the overall mean is (144/100) × [1 + 24 × 0.20] = 8.352 dollars², so its actual model SE is √8.352 ≈ $2.89. The individual receipts have not become more variable; their information overlaps.

Swipe the diagram sideways, or focus it and use the arrow keys. The same values are stated below.

Predict: if the retailer can collect 300 more receipts, which halves this model’s SE: taking 100 receipts in each of the same four stores, or taking 25 receipts in each of 16 independent stores?

Compare the two ways of reaching 400 rows

More within the same four stores: variance = (144/400) × [1 + 99 × 0.20] = 7.488 dollars², giving SE ≈ $2.74. More independent stores: variance = (144/400) × [1 + 24 × 0.20] = 2.088 dollars², giving SE ≈ $1.44, half the original unrounded SE. Both files contain 400 receipts; their sampling designs are different.

A way to see why: the model splits each receipt’s 144 dollars² of variance into a store-shared component of 28.8 and an individual component of 115.2. With J independent stores and m receipts per store, the overall mean’s variance is 28.8/J + 115.2/(Jm). More receipts within a store reduce the second term, while more independent stores reduce both.

Use the design, not a correction guessed from the file. Here the SD and correlation are supplied model values, not estimates from four stores. Real clustered data need a design-appropriate uncertainty method; four clusters are very little information for estimating between-store uncertainty. Unequal store sizes or a different target may need weights. This calculation explains dependence, not a universal SE formula for every business dataset.

11Check the model, not just the calculatorGraphical diagnostics and cautious conclusions+

A precise calculation can still answer the wrong question

The director now has a probability, but it is conditional on a sampling model. A week dominated by one outage may contain many records and little independent information. Before recommending a staffing change, inspect how the data were collected as well as how they look. A shape check cannot repair a design problem.

Check design before shape

Were observations selected representatively? Are repeated records, shared stores, time trends or common outages creating dependence? A bell-shaped histogram does not prove independence or rule out selection bias.

Look at the data

Use a histogram and boxplot for skewness and outliers. A normal Q-Q plot compares ordered observations with theoretical normal quantiles; a roughly straight line supports the shape assumption, while bends or extreme points suggest departures. The line need not have slope 1 or intercept 0 for unstandardized data.

Use numerical diagnostics with care

Normal skewness is 0 and ordinary kurtosis is 3, equivalent to excess kurtosis 0. Matching those two moments does not prove normality. The Jarque–Bera test combines deviations in skewness and kurtosis; it has limited small-sample power and an asymptotic reference distribution. Nonrejection is not confirmation. Very large samples can flag small, practically unimportant deviations.

Put the director’s recommendation together

Class 3 modeled an individual outcome. You have now modeled a statistic from many outcomes: its center, its standard error and, when justified, its reference distribution. The next class reverses the question. Instead of assuming a population mean and asking what samples could occur, estimation uses a sample to build a range for the unknown mean. Hypothesis testing then asks whether the sample conflicts with a specified benchmark.

Transfer task · A wider sample or more simulation?

The support director wants to halve the uncertainty in an average based on 25 independent tickets. A colleague proposes keeping those 25 tickets and simulating 10,000 averages. Another proposes sampling 100 independent tickets from the same population. Which addresses the goal? What if all 100 tickets come from one outage?

Reason through the answer

Under the same IID model, SE is σ/√n, so moving from 25 to 100 halves it. Increasing simulation repetitions only improves our picture of the sampling distribution; it adds no real information to the estimate from 25 tickets. Tickets from one outage may be correlated and unrepresentative, so 100 records do not automatically provide 100 independent observations. Improve the design before claiming the smaller SE.

Explain before moving on

A manager says, “The ticket histogram is skewed, so no average can be approximately normal.” Correct the claim and give one caution.

Compare your explanation

The histogram of individual observations and the sampling distribution of their average are different distributions. Under suitable IID finite-variance conditions, standardized averages can be approximately normal even when individual tickets are skewed. Strong skewness may require larger samples, and dependence can invalidate the simple calculation. A sample-size rule alone is not enough.

Written practice and solutions
  1. Sampling without replacement from a finite population is dependent; use the finite-population correction when appropriate.
  2. The sampling distribution describes a statistic over repeated samples, not the individual observed values.
  3. Unbiasedness is a repeated-sampling expectation, not a promise that this sample equals the truth.
  4. Quadrupling n halves the standard error under the same IID model.
  5. The LLN does not require a realized running average to improve after every observation.
  6. The CLT concerns the standardized sample mean and requires conditions; n=35 is not a guarantee.
  7. Increasing simulation repetitions B smooths the picture; increasing n changes the sampling distribution.
  8. K<20 means K≤19, with a normal continuity-correction boundary of 19.5.
  9. With n=16, the normal-sample t pivot has 15 degrees of freedom.
  10. A t p-value is a probability under a benchmark model, not the probability the benchmark is true or a predictive probability of a future raw mean.
