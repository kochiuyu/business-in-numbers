YOUR LEARNING PATH

Explain why an accurate alert can still be wrong.

The retailer now wants to review uncertain orders. Class 1 taught us to count and summarize observed records. Here we specify a model for what might happen to a randomly selected order. Our running question is whether an alert is convincing enough to justify investigation.

Bring with you: fractions, percentages and the idea of a count divided by a stated total. A decimal probability of .05 is 5%, or 5 out of 100 in an expected-count illustration. You do not need calculus or coding.

  1. Start with the alert lab and reason using counts.
  2. Follow events → overlap → conditional denominators → weighted scenarios → Bayes. These steps build the alert calculation rather than introducing unrelated formulas.
  3. Use odds for repeated evidence and counting for equally likely possibilities.
  4. Finish by explaining a new alert rate and naming the assumptions behind it.

01 / THE ALERT LAB

Predict → change → explain

Imagine an order-review system. A “real issue” means an order genuinely needs investigation. These rates are synthetic assumptions, not a measured system. Before moving a slider, predict whether a rarer issue makes an alert more or less convincing.

Work the default case before moving the sliders.

Start with 10,000 orders. A 1% base rate gives 100 real issues and 9,900 ordinary orders. Sensitivity catches 90 of the 100 issues. The 5% false-positive rate also flags 495 ordinary orders. The question “issue given alert” keeps only the 90 + 495 alerted orders, so its denominator is 585.

  1. Predict: hold the system’s two detection rates fixed. Will raising the issue base rate make a positive alert more convincing?

1. Define the possibilities before assigning their probabilities.

“What is the chance of an issue?” is incomplete until we say which orders, from which period, and how an order is selected. For the alert model, we select one order and record its true state and its alert status. Those two descriptions create four possible cases. An event can include several cases: “alert” includes both true and false alerts.

A die supplies a smaller example where every outcome can be listed. Equal likelihood makes counting easy there; business outcomes need not be equally likely. Do not confuse a model that assumes a rate with data that estimate that rate.

01Outcomes, events and probabilityBe precise about what is random and what is being counted+

A random experiment has an uncertain result: inspect a randomly selected order, toss a coin or roll a die. An outcome is one possible result. The sample space S contains all possible outcomes. An event is a set of outcomes, such as “the delivery is late.” Define the population, time period and selection process before attaching a probability.

For one fair die, S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. The event E = “even” is {2, 4, 6}; L = “greater than 3” is {4, 5, 6}. Each has probability 3/6 = 1/2.

{{math:classical}}

Classical probability counts equally likely outcomes. Relative frequency estimates probability from repeated observations: event count ÷ trial count. A fair-coin model says P(heads) = 1/2; a run of 100 tosses need not contain exactly 50 heads. Under independent, identically distributed trials, relative frequency tends to the model probability in the long run. Real order rates can change over time.

Technical depth · A model is more than a fraction

The axiomatic framework describes a probability measure on an allowed collection of events. It applies to finite, countably infinite and continuous sample spaces; probabilities can be informed by symmetry, data or a justified model. Not every outcome has the same probability. Do not use favorable-count ÷ total-count for unequal outcomes, or infer a valid model just from repeatability.

2. Translate “and,” “or” and “not” into groups of cases.

The manager may ask for orders that are late and damaged, late or damaged, or not late. These ask for an overlap, a combined group including the overlap, and everything outside a group. Draw or list the cases before calculating. “Or” in this course includes cases where both happen.

In the die activity below, compare “E and L” with “E or L,” then “E given L.” The first two choose outcomes from all six. The last restricts the universe to 4, 5 and 6 before asking which are even. The answer changes because the question changes.

02Combine events without double-countingIntersection, union, complement and partitions+

And · intersection

E ∩ L contains outcomes in both: {4, 6}. Its probability is 2/6.

Or · union

E ∪ L contains outcomes in either or both: {2, 4, 5, 6}. Its probability is 4/6.

Not · complement

Eᶜ contains outcomes in S but not E: {1, 3, 5}. Its probability is 3/6.

See the event, then the fraction.

Selected outcomes have a solid border and a check. Outcomes outside a conditional sample space are crossed out. The original event E remains {2, 4, 6}; conditioning restricts which outcomes are possible.

Three different kinds of grouping

Mutually exclusive: at most one event can happen. Odd and even on one die do not overlap. Collectively exhaustive: at least one must happen. Odd {1, 3, 5}, prime {2, 3, 5} and {4, 6} cover S but overlap. A partition: exactly one happens, so the events are pairwise disjoint and exhaustive. Odd/even is a partition.

For a business example, “delivered on time” and “delivered late” form a partition only when every order in the defined sample has been delivered and the deadline is defined. Pending or cancelled orders need their own treatment.

Technical depth · Set language

A ∩ B = ∅ means disjoint; A ∪ B = S means exhaustive for two events. For a partition H₁, …, Hₖ, all pairs Hᵢ ∩ Hⱼ are empty for i ≠ j and their union is S. Standard set partitions use nonempty subsets. Complements always refer to a specified S. “Exhaustive” does not mean that all the events occur at once.

3. Count the overlap once, not twice.

Suppose we count every express order and every on-time order. Express orders that were also on time enter both counts. Subtract that overlap once to count each qualifying order once: in the 80-order table, 40 + 35 − 15 = 60. Dividing by 80 gives .75. This is the addition rule in words before symbols.

The complement is a second shortcut: if exactly one of “late” and “not late” happens, their probabilities total 1. Always check the sample space. “Not late” may include pending orders unless the data are restricted to completed deliveries.

03The rules probability must obeyAxioms, complements, addition and a validity check+

Probabilities are between zero and one; P(S) = 1; probabilities add for disjoint events. These are constraints, not optional conventions. For example, the probability of an order being both late and damaged cannot exceed the probability of it being late.

{{math:complement}}

Sometimes “not” is easier. On a fair die, P(less than 6) = 1 − 1/6 = 5/6. For two independent fair dice, P(sum less than 12) = 1 − P(6,6) = 35/36.

{{math:addition}}

For the die events E and L: 1/2 + 1/2 − 2/6 = 2/3. Subtract the overlap because adding both events counted it twice. For mutually exclusive events the overlap is zero.

Technical depth · Axioms and consequences

{{math:axioms}}

The final identity requires pairwise disjoint events and a countable union. Finite additivity follows by taking the remaining events empty. The upper bound of 1 follows from nonnegativity and normalization. Do not sum individual zero-probability points over an uncountable continuous space as if this were countable additivity.

Complement proof: A and Aᶜ partition S, so P(A) + P(Aᶜ) = P(S) = 1. Addition proof: split B into the part outside A and the overlap. Add the disjoint pieces once.

{{math:bounds}}

Spot the impossible input: the supplied source gives P(A) = 0.2, P(B) = 0.5, P(A ∩ B) = 0.3. This is not a valid probability model because 0.3 > 0.2. Dividing 0.3 by 0.5 does not repair it. A consistent replacement is P(A) = 0.4 with the same other two values; then P(A | B) = 0.6.

4. New information changes who is in the denominator.

Picture covering every row except express orders. There are now 40 relevant orders, of which 15 were on time: 15/40. If instead you cover all orders except on-time ones, 35 remain and 15 are express: 15/35. The same overlap produces two different conditional probabilities because the information after “given” selects a different group.

This also explains multiplication: 40/80 of all orders are express, and 15/40 of those are on time. Multiplying cancels the intermediate group and gives 15/80 for the joint event. Replacing a conditional rate with a general rate requires an independence assumption.

04Conditioning changes the denominatorJoint, marginal and conditional probabilities answer different questions+

“Given B” means we restrict attention to cases where B is true and renormalize their probability to one. In the table, P(on time) = 35/80, but P(on time | express) = 15/40 = 0.375. The denominator is express orders, not all orders.

{{math:conditional}}

Reverse the question and the denominator changes: P(express | on time) = 15/35 ≈ 0.4286. Neither conditional equals the joint probability P(express and on time) = 15/80 = 0.1875. A row or column total divided by the grand total is a marginal probability.

For a fair die, given L = {4, 5, 6}, only three equally likely outcomes remain. Two are even, so P(E | L) = 2/3. If P(B) = 0, the elementary ratio is undefined. An impossible conditioning event does not give a conditional probability of zero.

{{math:multiply}}

Without replacement, the second draw changes.

A bag contains 3 green and 3 red balls. Draw two uniformly without replacement. P(first green) = 3/6. Given first green, P(second green) = 2/5. Therefore P(both green) = (3/6)(2/5) = 1/5. With replacement and a fresh random draw, it would be (3/6)(3/6) = 1/4.

Technical depth · Special cases and chains

If A and B are disjoint and P(B) > 0, P(A | B) = 0. If B is a subset of A and P(B) > 0, P(A | B) = 1. Neither fact changes the definition of A itself.

{{math:chain}}

Multiply along a path using the information accumulated so far. A frequent mistake is replacing a conditional factor with an unconditional one without establishing independence.

5. Reassemble the whole from non-overlapping routes.

Conditioning zoomed into one group. Total probability zooms back out. Imagine 1,000 orders: warehouse A handles 600 and makes 12 late; B handles 400 and makes 32 late. Overall, 44/1,000 = 4.4% are late. The smaller warehouse’s 8% rate must not receive the same weight as the larger warehouse’s 2% rate.

In a tree, multiply along a route to obtain its share of the whole, then add the routes ending in the event you want. For alerts, the two routes are real issue → alert and no issue → alert. Both belong in the eventual denominator.

05Average across scenarios correctlyTotal probability, weighted rates and tree diagrams+

Suppose warehouse A handles 60% of orders with a 2% late rate, while B handles 40% with an 8% late rate. Overall late probability is 0.6 × 0.02 + 0.4 × 0.08 = 0.044, or 4.4%. It is not the unweighted average of 2% and 8%.

{{math:total}}

The scenarios must partition the possibilities. Start a tree with scenario probabilities, multiply along each path, then sum the paths that produce the event. In the alert lab, “issue” and “no issue” are the scenarios. Both can lead to an alert.

Technical depth · Partition decomposition

For a partition Hᵢ, the intersections B ∩ Hᵢ are disjoint and their union is B. Therefore P(B) = Σ P(B ∩ Hᵢ). Apply the multiplication rule to each term with P(Hᵢ) > 0. A zero-probability scenario contributes zero without requiring an undefined conditional ratio.

The same structure supports scenario planning, including demand or economic scenarios. It gives a model-based probability, not a guaranteed forecast. A good/bad-news partition must really cover all possibilities, and the conditional rates must be defensible.

6. Ask whether knowing one event changes the other.

Independence means information leaves a probability unchanged. It does not mean that two events cannot happen together. In fact, learning that an order was delivered on time rules out “late,” so these positive-probability events are dependent. Independent events can overlap.

Pause and explain: An analyst multiplies the overall probabilities of two branches failing. What must be justified first?

Compare your reasoning

That the failures are independent under the model. A shared outage, supplier or weather shock could connect them. Without independence, use the probability of the second failure conditional on the first, not its overall probability.

06Independence is not mutual exclusivity“No information” is different from “cannot both happen”+

Two events are independent if learning one does not change the probability of the other. The product definition also handles zero-probability events:

{{math:independent}}

When P(B) > 0, this is equivalent to P(A | B) = P(A). When P(A) > 0, it is equivalent to P(B | A) = P(B). Given P(A) = 0.6, P(B) = 0.5 and overlap 0.25, the events are not independent: 0.6 × 0.5 = 0.3 ≠ 0.25.

Mutually exclusive events cannot occur together. If both have positive probability, observing one makes the other impossible, so they are dependent. Odd and even on one die are mutually exclusive. A fair die outcome and a separately randomized coin outcome can be independent, but they can happen together.

Technical depth · Assumptions behind multiplication

Replacement restores the composition of a bag. Independent randomization of each draw is also needed; replacement alone does not force independence. For several events, pairwise independence is weaker than mutual independence. Multiplying all marginal probabilities requires the stronger joint assumption, not just separate pair checks.

Under independence, P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A)P(B). For mutually exclusive events it is P(A) + P(B). Both properties can hold when at least one event has probability zero. An observed sample near the product rule is not proof that a business process is independent.

7. Reverse the question by keeping only the evidence.

We now have every ingredient for Bayes: multiplication builds the true-alert route, total probability adds all alert routes, and conditioning divides the first by the second. Start with 90 true alerts out of 585 alerts. Bayes is this same calculation written in probabilities, not a new kind of arithmetic.

Read the vertical bar as “among cases with.” P(alert | issue) asks among issues, how many alert? P(issue | alert) asks among alerts, how many are issues? Reversing those words without changing the denominator is the base-rate trap.

07Bayes: turn evidence aroundPrior, likelihood, marginal and posterior+

We often know how a signal behaves if a condition is true, but need the probability of the condition given the signal. Bayes’ rule reverses the direction without confusing the two conditionals. Walk through the probability tree to see the numerator, denominator and updated share.

  • Prior P(H): the probability before this evidence.
  • Likelihood P(+ | H): how likely the evidence is under the hypothesis.
  • Marginal P(+): the probability of the evidence across all scenarios.
  • Posterior P(H | +): the updated probability after the evidence.
{{math:bayes}}{{math:bayes-numbers}}

The alert system’s sensitivity is P(+ | H) = 90%. Its specificity is P(− | Hᶜ) = 95%, so the false-positive rate is 5%. The false-negative rate is 10%. Positive predictive value is P(H | +), about 15.38% here. The false-positive rate is not the fraction of alerts that are false.

For the two bags, observing green favors A, but does not identify it with certainty:

{{math:bags}}

P(B | G) = 3/8 and the posteriors sum to 1. Counting 5 of the 8 green balls works here because the bags have equal selection probabilities and equal sizes. If those change, use weighted probabilities rather than pooling ball counts.

Revisit the source’s screening example

In the source’s hypothetical model, prior = 0.4, sensitivity = 0.8 and false-positive rate = 0.4. Then P(+) = 0.56, P(H | +) = 4/7 ≈ 0.5714, and P(H | −) = 2/11 ≈ 0.1818. A positive raises probability by 17.14 percentage points (42.86% relative); a negative lowers it by 21.82 percentage points (54.55% relative). These are teaching assumptions, not clinical advice.

The terms Type I and Type II error belong to a specified hypothesis-testing setup. False positive corresponds to Type I when the null is “condition absent”; false negative corresponds to Type II under that convention. They are not unconditional error probabilities.

Technical depth · Derivation and multiple hypotheses

Start with P(H | B) = P(H ∩ B)/P(B). Replace the numerator by P(B | H)P(H), then obtain the denominator using total probability.

{{math:general-bayes}}

For a fixed observed B, the posterior is proportional to likelihood × prior across the hypotheses. The marginal is the normalization constant, but it depends on the prior and the model. It is not independent of prior beliefs.

When 0 < P(H) and P(B) > 0, the ratio P(B | H)/P(B) tells how the posterior compares with the prior: above one raises it, below one lowers it, and one leaves it unchanged. Zero prior remains zero in ordinary Bayes updating; an observation with zero marginal probability is outside the assumed model.

8. Repeated evidence requires a fresh update, not a copied percentage.

A probability compares successes with all possibilities. Odds compare successes with failures: probability 1/5 means one success for four failures, or odds 1:4. Updating odds can simplify repeated evidence, but the simplification still depends on how the signals were generated.

In the source model below, a positive is twice as likely for a true condition as for an absent one. It doubles the odds, not the probability. A second independent signal within each true state doubles those odds again. Reading the same report twice supplies no second observation.

08Odds and repeated evidenceUpdate odds, not probability with a fixed multiplier+

A probability of 0.2 corresponds to odds in favor of 1:4 and odds against of 4:1. Odds compare occurrence with non-occurrence; probability compares occurrence with all possibilities.

{{math:odds}}

Odds are zero at p = 0, increase without bound as p approaches 1, and have no finite value at p = 1. An odds ratio compares the odds of two groups or conditions. A single value p/(1 − p) is odds, not an odds ratio.

The source’s fair-payoff illustration

A hypothetical fair payoff with net winnings of $4 for a $1 loss has break-even probability 1/5: 4p − (1 − p) = 0. A die result of six has odds in favor 1:5 and a fair net payoff of 5:1. A quoted payout is not evidence that the true event probability equals its implied fair probability. This is an illustration of expected payoff, not betting advice.

Why the same evidence does not add the same percentage.

In the source model (prior 40%, sensitivity 80%, false-positive rate 40%), one positive gives 4/7 ≈ 57.14%. If two results are independent conditional on the true state, two positives give 8/11 ≈ 72.73%, not 81.63%. Recompute the probability of the next signal after each update.

{{math:two-tests}}

Repeating a report or reusing the same evidence is not an independent test. Shared equipment, shared data or shared errors can make repeated signals dependent. Then use the joint or conditional likelihoods for that dependence.

Technical depth · Odds accumulate likelihood ratios

{{math:odds-bayes}}

Here a positive has likelihood ratio 0.8/0.4 = 2. Prior odds 2/3 become 4/3 after one positive and 8/3 after two. Converting 8/3 back to probability gives 8/11. The odds multiplier can stay fixed under the assumptions, while the probability multiplier changes.

{{math:second-marginal}}{{math:repeated}}

For this model, one negative gives 2/11 ≈ 18.18%, two negatives give 2/29 ≈ 6.90%, and three give 2/83 ≈ 2.41%. These are not successive halvings. The formula assumes constant rates and conditional independence within each true state; the denominator must be positive. It describes a specified result sequence. For unordered counts the common combinatorial factor cancels in the posterior.

9. Count the right objects before treating them as equally likely.

Assigning three distinct roles is different from choosing a three-person team. The same team has 3 × 2 × 1 = 6 assignments, so counting assignments overcounts teams sixfold. That is why combinations divide permutations by 3!, rather than an arbitrary rule to memorize.

Use the calculator with n = 5 and r = 3: predict that ordered selection without replacement gives 60 while an unordered committee gives 10. Then consider replacement: allowing a choice again gives 5 × 5 × 5 = 125 sequences. Only after defining what each outcome is can equal-likelihood counts become probabilities.

Two dice are not eleven equally likely sums.

Two independent fair dice have 6 × 6 = 36 equally likely ordered pairs. (1,2) differs from (2,1). Sum less than 4 has three pairs: (1,1), (1,2), (2,1), so its probability is 1/12. Sum less than 11 excludes (5,6), (6,5), (6,6), so it has probability 33/36 = 11/12.

Exactly two tails in four tosses

Choose the two tail positions. The six sequences are TTHH, THTH, THHT, HTTH, HTHT and HHTT. Under independent fair tosses they are equally likely.

Technical depth · Why divide by r!?

Every selected set of r distinct objects appears in r! different orders. Dividing nPr by r! counts each unordered set once. For all n objects, there are n! permutations. Repeated identical objects, constrained menus, or unordered selections with replacement require different counting arguments; do not apply nPr or nCr by appearance alone.

The events “sum = 2” through “sum = 12” partition the two-dice sample space, but their probabilities differ. Counting possible sums as if each had probability 1/11 is incorrect. The product count of 36 alone also does not guarantee uniform probabilities; the fair and independent dice assumptions do that.

Technical depth · Validation across tools

Validate integer, nonnegative counts before applying counting formulas. Excel counting functions can truncate noninteger arguments, while Python integer combinatorial functions reject them. The tools also differ for some out-of-range inputs. Do not rely on those differences to validate a model.

For a 2 × 2 count table, cells must be nonnegative and the total positive. For event probabilities, check the intersection bounds. For Bayes, rates must lie in [0,1] and the evidence must have positive probability. Avoid replacing an undefined conditional with zero or a blank that silently enters later calculations.

Make one change: keep sensitivity and false-positive rate fixed, but increase the base rate from 1% to 20%. Predict the direction, compute the answer in the lab and one tool, then explain why the same system now yields a different probability after an alert.

Transfer: explain the alert to a manager.

Say what was believed before the signal, what group the signal selects, and what fraction of that group has the issue. Then separate that probability from the action: the costs of investigation, false alarms and missed issues still matter. A larger posterior is evidence for review, not proof of wrongdoing.

Pause and explain: A new region has a 2% issue base rate, with sensitivity still 90% and false-positive rate still 5%. Among 10,000 expected orders, what is the probability of a real issue given an alert?

Compare your reasoning

There are 200 issues and 9,800 non-issues. Expected true alerts: 200 × .90 = 180. Expected false alerts: 9,800 × .05 = 490. Keep all 670 alerts, giving 180/670 ≈ 26.87%. This is higher than the 1%-base-rate result even though the detector is unchanged. Applying those detection rates to a new region is an assumption that should be checked.

Next, suppose we need staffing for the number of alerted orders rather than a probability for one order. That requires the whole range of possible counts, which is the purpose of Class 3.
