One question. One useful chart.

Before calculating, choose a view that could reveal what a summary might hide. The order experiment in the mean step will return to these ideas. First ask what one observation represents: a category, one numerical value, a time period, or a pair of measurements. That choice determines what positions and lengths in a graph should mean. Work through the five questions below from left to right. Every example uses synthetic business data; each view has a different business purpose.

Read the axes before reading the story.

A tall bar might mean many orders, a large order value, or a high frequency density. Those are different quantities. Read the labels and units first, identify what one bar or point represents, then describe one pattern in words. Finally ask what the graph cannot establish. Bar chart or histogram?

A histogram groups one numerical variable into intervals called bins. Adjacent bins touch because the numeric scale is continuous. Each bar here counts deliveries within that interval. Do not reorder the bins by frequency.

A bar chart compares separate categories, with gaps between bars. The order-book chart has one bar per order ID. It shows individual purchases; a histogram instead asks how often values fall into ranges.

Try the bin widths: narrower bins show detail but may look noisy; wider bins may conceal clusters or gaps. These data concentrate around 10–20 minutes, with a few longer deliveries, including 38 minutes. Small samples do not establish the population's shape.

A frequency polygon joins the frequency at each bin midpoint: at width 10, the points are (5, 4), (15, 13), (25, 2), (35, 1). It describes the distribution, not changes over time. This overlay joins only observed-bin midpoints; some textbooks add zero-frequency end points.

Technical depth · Unequal bin widths

For equal-width bins, frequency heights compare counts directly. With unequal widths, use frequency density = count / bin width, so each bar's area represents frequency. For relative-frequency density, divide by the observation count as well; the total bar area is 1. A taller density bar does not necessarily contain more observations.

Read January to June and identify both the overall rise and the two local falls. These can be true at the same time: “rising overall” does not mean “rising every month.”

Read the graph

Revenue rises overall from $40,000 in January to $60,000 in June, but falls in March and June. A line reveals that sequence; ranking the months by sales would destroy it.

A time-series line chart places time on the horizontal axis and a numerical measure on the vertical axis. Keep elapsed-time spacing accurate and mark missing periods rather than silently treating them as zero.

Start by checking trend, changes and unusual periods. Six months are not enough to establish a recurring seasonal pattern. Connecting points helps us follow sequence; it does not mean we observed every moment between them.

Return to Step 7: the unusual-order decision →

Read the middle. Investigate the extremes.

Return to the order values. A boxplot compresses their distribution while keeping the middle and unusual extremes visible. First locate the median at $31, then the box containing the middle half, then the isolated $200 order. Change the quartile convention and observe that the box edges move slightly but the underlying purchases do not. The spread lesson will explain how these cut points are calculated.

Q₁ = $24.25; median = $31.00; Q₃ = $41.00; IQR = $16.75. Fences: −$0.88 to $66.13. Whiskers: $18 and $46. The $200 order is flagged.

Five things to read

  1. The box runs from the first quartile, Q₁, to the third, Q₃: the middle half of the distribution.
  2. The line inside marks the median.
  3. The box width here is the interquartile range, IQR = Q₃ − Q₁.
  4. For this modified boxplot, whiskers end at the most extreme observed values still inside the 1.5 × IQR fences.
  5. Separate points beyond the fences are flags to investigate, not automatic errors.

With the default exclusive quartiles, the upper fence is $66.125, but the upper whisker ends at an actual observation, $46. The maximum is still $200. Some boxplots instead use minimum-to-maximum whiskers: always state the convention.

Boxplots make medians and spreads across groups easy to compare. They hide individual detail and multiple peaks; use a histogram or the raw observations too. The spread lesson explains quartiles and IQR.

1 / Match the question

Categories → bars. One numerical distribution → histogram or boxplot. Time → line. Two paired numerical variables → scatter. A simple share of one whole → pie, if useful.

2 / Show the scale honestly

Bar lengths need a zero baseline. Line and scatter axes need not always start at zero, but label their limits clearly. Compare groups on consistent scales and avoid decorative 3D effects.

3 / Tell the reader what was measured

Give a meaningful title, labels, units, period and sample size. Distinguish counts, percentages and money. Include labels or a data table so color is not the only way to read the chart.

Make the histogram counts match exactly

  1. Enter the 20 delivery times in A2:A21. Put lower boundaries 0, 10, 20, 30 in D2:D5 and upper boundaries 10, 20, 30, 40 in E2:E5.
  2. In F2, enter =COUNTIFS($A$2:$A$21,">="&D2,$A$2:$A$21,"<"&E2). Fill down through F4.
  3. For the final bin, use =COUNTIFS($A$2:$A$21,">="&D5,$A$2:$A$21,"<="&E5) in F5. Counts should be 4, 13, 2, 1. Use =F2/COUNT($A$2:$A$21) for relative frequency, format as a percentage and fill down.
  4. Create interval labels 0–<10, 10–<20, 20–<30, 30–40 inclusive. Select labels and counts → Insert → Clustered Column. Set Gap Width to 0% and keep bins in numerical order. This works because all bins have equal widths.
  5. For the polygon, compute midpoints with =(D2+E2)/2, fill down and use an XY scatter chart with straight lines and markers, with midpoint as x and count as y.

Boundary check: Excel's built-in histogram and FREQUENCY commonly use upper-inclusive bins; the explicit table here and Matplotlib use lower-inclusive bins, with the last upper boundary included. A delivery exactly at 10 can land in a different bin. Check the rule before concluding one tool is wrong.
