01Know your dataFull explanation · data types, tables and practice+

Start with a row, not a formula.

The manager has three files, but the rows mean different things. An order value describes a purchase; delivery days describe a shipment; an advertising/sales pair describes a branch. Identify that unit first, then decide what can sensibly be counted, averaged or compared.

What kind of value is in the column?

A variable is a characteristic recorded for each unit. Some values name categories, some put categories in order, and some measure an amount. A payment-method code of 1, 2 or 3 names a category: averaging it does not produce a useful payment method. The distinctions below tell us which summaries make sense.

Descriptive statistics summarizes the observations you have. Inferential statistics uses a sample and assumptions to learn about a wider population. A chart alone does not make an inference reliable.

Technical depth · Measurement scales

On an interval scale, equal differences are meaningful but zero is arbitrary (Celsius temperature). On a ratio scale, zero is meaningful and ratios make sense (duration, mass, order value). Taking means of ordinal scores assumes more than order alone: justify the spacing assumption.

02Choose a centerFull explanation · calculations, order lab and weighted means+

“Typical” could mean two different things.

Finance needs revenue per order; a promotion planner may need the middle purchase. Learn what the mean and median measure, calculate both on the same twelve orders, then change just the large order to see why the answers differ.

Mean

Add the values, divide by their count.

{{math:mean}}

Uses every value and changes when a distant value changes. It preserves the total: n × mean = total.

Median

Sort the values and find the middle.

Odd n: one middle value
Even n, numeric data: average two middle values

Less sensitive to a few extreme values. It needs meaningful order. With ordinal categories, identify the middle category or categories rather than averaging category labels.

Mode

Find the value or category occurring most often.

{{math:mode}}

There may be several. Here, if every value occurs once, we report “no repeated mode,” matching Excel's no-repeat behavior.

Work through the original order book.

Sorted dollar values: 18, 22, 24, 25, 28, 30, 32, 35, 38, 42, 46, 200.

  1. Recover revenue per order. The twelve values total $540. Redistribute that total equally: $540 ÷ 12 = $45.
  2. Locate the middle purchase. With twelve values, the middle lies between positions 6 and 7. Their midpoint is ($30 + $32) ÷ 2 = $31.
  3. Check repetition. Every value occurs once, so this record has no repeated mode.

The $45 mean reconstructs revenue exactly when multiplied by twelve. The $31 median describes the middle of the ordered purchases. Neither calculation tells us which price would maximize future sales.

Technical depth · Not every average is arithmetic

Weighted mean: {{math:weighted-mean}}. With nonnegative weights and a positive total weight, observations can represent different quantities. For values 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and weights 6, 3, 2, 3, 2: 40/16 = 2.5. For grouped frequencies, sum over distinct values, not every observation again.

Geometric mean: {{math:geometric-mean}}, for positive values. For compound returns, use growth factors 1 + r, then subtract 1. Two one-year returns of 10% and 90% give 1.1 × 1.9 − 1 = 109% total growth, versus 125% for two 50% returns. The annual compound rate for the first option is √2.09 − 1 ≈ 44.57%.

Harmonic mean: {{math:harmonic-mean}}, generally for positive rates with equal numerator amounts. Equal-distance trips at 30 and 60 km/h average 40 km/h. The harmonic mean of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 is 2.1898. The weighting structure must match the question.

Outlier handling: Trimming removes observations under a stated rule; winsorizing caps values at stated thresholds. For 1, 2, 3, 4, 50, removing 50 gives 2.5; replacing 50 with 4 gives 2.8. These are illustrations, not recommendations. Check data quality, justify the rule and report the effect.

The mean balances signed deviations: {{math:balance}}. It minimizes the sum of squared deviations; a median minimizes the sum of absolute deviations.

Count purchases, not branch labels

Predict first: If a $20 branch has three times as many orders as a $60 branch, should the combined mean be halfway between $20 and $60?

Same number of orders

A: 1 order × $20 = $20. B: 1 order × $60 = $60. Total $80 ÷ 2 orders = $40 per order.

Three orders versus one

A: 3 orders × $20 = $60. B: 1 order × $60 = $60. Total $120 ÷ 4 orders = $30 per order. The branch means did not change; the mix of purchases did.

Interpret what changed

Observe that $30 is closer to the busy branch’s $20. Each purchase gets one vote, not each branch. Averaging the two means gives $40 only when their order counts are equal. Before combining reported averages, ask for the count behind each one. These two cases are separate from the retailer’s 12-order record.

Follow the money through two years

Predict first: Both plans average 50% when you add their yearly percentage returns and divide by two. Will they finish with the same amount?

10%, then 90%

$100 × 1.10 = $110; then $110 × 1.90 = $209. Total growth is 109%. The constant yearly rate giving the same endpoint is √2.09 − 1 ≈ 44.57%.

50%, then 50%

$100 × 1.50 = $150; then $150 × 1.50 = $225. Total growth is 125%. The constant yearly rate is 50%.

Operations sees equal means. Your job is to check the variation and the deliveries that miss the target.

{{visual:spread}}

TRY A SMALL COMPARISON · SEPARATE TEACHING EXAMPLE

Why square the gaps from the mean?

Predict first: Deliveries of 4, 5 and 6 days average 5 days. Does a zero sum of signed gaps mean there is no variation?

Keep signed gaps

Early and late deliveries cancel: −1 + 0 + 1 = 0 days. Signed gaps balance around a mean even when the observations differ.

Square the gaps

Squared gaps total 2 days². If these are the entire population, variance = 2/3 days² and SD ≈ 0.816 days. If they are a sample used to estimate population variance, s² = 2/(3−1) = 1 day² and s = 1 day.

Interpret what changed

Squaring makes both directions count toward variation and gives larger gaps more influence. Taking the square root returns to days. Decide whether you have a population or a sample before choosing n or n−1. This separate three-delivery example does not change the supplier records.

CONNECT THE STEPS · WORKED INTERPRETATION

Why does a sample variance divide by n − 1?

Use Supplier A’s same five deliveries: 4, 5, 5, 5, 6 days. We calculated the mean from these records, so the deviations must balance around that fitted mean.

The squared deviations total 2 days². To describe just these five records, divide by 5: variance = 0.4 days² and SD ≈ 0.63 days. To estimate a wider population’s variance from an independent, identically distributed sample, divide by 4: sample variance = 0.5 days² and sample SD ≈ 0.71 days.

Fitting the mean to the sample tends to make the squared distances too small for estimating population variance. The n − 1 correction makes sample variance unbiased under those assumptions; it does not make sample SD exactly unbiased. It cannot repair biased sampling or turn five deliveries into a reliable forecast.

Check: do we remove the fifth delivery?

No. We still add all five squared deviations. “Four degrees of freedom” describes the constraint after fitting the mean, not four remaining observations.

A quartile cuts the sorted data near a quarter of the way through. Q₁ and Q₃ bound the middle half; their difference is the IQR. “Exclusive” and “inclusive” are different interpolation rules, so they can give different quartiles on the same values. The technical panel below works through those ranks.

Change one convention: keep the order values fixed and select “Treat the lab data as a sample.” The mean does not change, but the variance denominator changes from n to n − 1, so the reported SD increases. Technical depth · Quartiles and boxplots

Sort the n observations. The exclusive convention interpolates at rank p(n + 1). The inclusive convention interpolates at rank 1 + p(n − 1). With data 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6: exclusive Q₁ = 1.75 and Q₃ = 5.25; inclusive Q₁ = 2.25 and Q₃ = 4.75. The results differ by convention, not a calculation error.

For rank k + α, use {{math:interpolation}}. Exclusive percentiles are not defined when the requested rank lies outside the observed positions. This lab accepts at least four observations so its two exclusive quartiles exist.

A five-number summary is minimum, Q₁, median, Q₃, maximum. Some boxplots draw whiskers to min/max; a common alternative ends whiskers at the most extreme observed values within 1.5 × IQR of the box and plots the remaining points separately. Always identify which version is used.

Population variance: {{math:population-variance}}. Sample variance: {{math:sample-variance}}. Using the sample mean consumes one degree of freedom; under independent, identically distributed sampling with finite variance, the n − 1 version is unbiased for population variance. The sample SD itself is not generally unbiased.

Check that a larger number means more variation, not a new unit.

A’s SD of 0.6325 days becomes about 15.18 hours when multiplied by 24. Its mean also becomes 120 hours. The deliveries did not change. Mean and SD scale by the same positive conversion factor; variance scales by its square.

The coefficient of variation (CV) is SD ÷ mean × 100%. For A it is about 12.65% in either days or hours. This relative measure is useful for ratio-scale data with a positive, meaningful mean and becomes unstable near zero. Build the box and its review fences.

  1. Find the middle half. On the original twelve ordered values, exclusive quartiles use ranks 0.25 × 13 = 3.25 and 0.75 × 13 = 9.75. A quarter of the way from $24 to $25 is Q₁ = $24.25; three quarters of the way from $38 to $42 is Q₃ = $41.
  2. Measure its width. IQR = $41 − $24.25 = $16.75.
  3. Extend the review range. 1.5 × $16.75 = $25.125. The lower fence is $24.25 − $25.125 = −$0.875; the upper fence is $41 + $25.125 = $66.125.
  4. Check observations, not just endpoints. $200 is above the upper fence; every other order is inside. The whiskers end at $18 and $46, the most extreme actual values inside the fences.

Read the long right side before summarizing its shape.

Check the invoice and order type. Correct a confirmed entry error; retain a genuine purchase, and consider describing order types separately if the distinction is meaningful. A review fence alone supplies no reason to delete valid revenue.

Use shape summaries to supplement the picture.

Skewness describes asymmetry through a standardized third moment. Positive skewness often goes with a longer right tail; negative skewness often goes with a longer left tail. Inspect the plot as well as the number.

Kurtosis uses the fourth power of deviations and is especially sensitive to extreme values. Excess kurtosis subtracts 3, the normal distribution's kurtosis. It is not simply a measure of peak height.

Why do the powers matter, and why standardize first?

A standardized deviation of −3 contributes −27 to the cube and 81 to the fourth power; +3 contributes +27 and the same 81. A deviation of one SD contributes just 1 in fourth power. Thus one observation three SDs from the mean contributes as much to the fourth-power total as 81 observations one SD away. The final average also depends on how many observations there are.

Changing dollars to cents multiplies deviations and SD by 100, leaving every z unchanged. Raw third and fourth central moments have units dollars³ and dollars⁴; division by SD³ and SD⁴ removes those units. Adding a fixed amount to every order also leaves these shape measures unchanged.

These comparisons hold for deviations on a given standardized scale. If you edit an order, the mean and SD change too: recompute all z values. A larger raw maximum does not automatically mean larger standardized kurtosis.

Make “zero” precise: two counterexamples you can check

Two numbers cannot certify a distribution’s shape.

Both order books below are synthetic, use the population-moment convention and can be checked without a calculator library. Choose a claim to inspect.

Zero skewness does not mean symmetry.

Orders: $7; five at $9; four at $12 (ten orders). The mean is $10; the median is $9. Deviations from the mean are −3 once, −1 five times and +2 four times.

The sum of cubes is −27 − 5 + 32 = 0, so skewness is zero. Yet there is no matching +3 deviation for −3, or +1 for −1: the values and their frequencies are not mirror images about the mean. Cancellation of cubes is one numerical condition, not a full description of the distribution.

Does symmetry imply zero skewness?

Yes, when the third moment exists and spread is nonzero: each equally weighted pair of opposite deviations has cubes that cancel. The implication only goes one way. Do not infer symmetry from zero skewness, or use mean = median as a universal skewness test.

Zero excess kurtosis does not mean normality.

Orders: $9, $10, $10, $10, $10, $11. The mean is $10. Deviations are −1, 0, 0, 0, 0, +1, so m₂ = 2 ÷ 6 = 1/3 and m₄ = 2 ÷ 6 = 1/3. Pearson kurtosis is (1/3) ÷ (1/3)² = 3; excess is 0.

If these three possible amounts have probabilities 1/6, 4/6 and 1/6, this is a discrete distribution with exactly zero excess, not a continuous normal distribution. Its population skewness is also zero. Even matching both shape numbers does not establish normality; six observed orders certainly cannot establish it either.

The normal benchmark is a convention, not a verdict. A normal population has Pearson kurtosis 3, so subtracting 3 makes its excess 0. Observed samples from it need not have excess exactly zero. Positive excess means a fourth standardized moment above this benchmark; negative excess means below it. It is not a probability of an extreme event, and it does not determine the whole tail.

Why not just “peakedness”? Kurtosis averages fourth powers of standardized deviations. Observations near the mean contribute little, while distant observations contribute strongly on either side. Peak height, bin width and the rest of the distribution can vary without a one-to-one change in kurtosis. Inspect the plot, the actual extreme records, the sample size and the convention before using a kurtosis number to describe business risk.

Two tempting shortcuts to avoid

Zero skewness does not prove symmetry or equality of mean and median. Zero excess kurtosis does not prove normality. A small dataset cannot reliably establish a population's tail behavior.

Technical depth · Standardized moments

A central moment is an average power of deviations from the mean. Here m₂ uses denominator n, σ = √m₂, and zᵢ = (xᵢ − x̄) ÷ σ. Thus g₁ is the average of zᵢ³ and Pearson kurtosis is the average of zᵢ⁴; g₂ below is excess. For observed records, these are descriptive population-version formulas; they do not claim that the records are the entire business population.

How the quartile code works

st.quantiles(...) returns three cut points: Q₁, the median and Q₃. q1, _, q3 = ... saves the first and last; the name _ marks the middle value we do not need here. Subtract Q₁ from Q₃ for the IQR. In Excel, the exclusive IQR is =QUARTILE.EXC(A2:A6,3)-QUARTILE.EXC(A2:A6,1).

Match the question, not just the function name

Population or sample? Use VAR.P / st.pvariance and STDEV.P / st.pstdev to describe the whole target population (divide by n). Use VAR.S / st.variance and STDEV.S / st.stdev to estimate population variation from a sample (variance divides by n − 1). The latter needs at least two values. Decide what population the data represent before choosing.

Exclusive or inclusive? Both are valid quartile conventions. We name the method explicitly in Python to match Excel. For these five values, exclusive gives an IQR of 3; inclusive gives 2. An unexplained difference is not necessarily a calculation error.

Technical depth · Python shape summaries

The standard-library module has no skewness or kurtosis function. This teaching function follows the formulas in the shape lesson: population moments, corrected sample skewness (Excel SKEW) and corrected sample excess kurtosis (Excel KURT). It expects finite numeric values and rejects fewer than four observations or constant data. Sample skewness alone needs three observations; the combined function requires four because it also calculates sample excess kurtosis.

Technical depth · Reconcile other libraries

NumPy’s var and std default to ddof=0 (divide by n). pandas Series.var and Series.std default to ddof=1 (divide by n − 1). NumPy’s default quantile method, linear, matches Excel’s inclusive interpolation; its weibull method matches exclusive interpolation where Excel defines the quantile. Python’s statistics.quantiles defaults to exclusive, so state the method explicitly. On very small datasets Python may extrapolate where Excel’s exclusive quartiles return an error. Use at least four observations for the quartile comparisons in this lesson.

Also check missing values, nonnumeric cells, finite inputs, paired-row alignment and bias corrections before comparing outputs. These examples deliberately use complete numeric data. Excel’s handling of blank/text cells is not the same as putting None or text into a Python list.

Technical depth · Build and weight it yourself

The Python counterpart to the manual population variance exercise is sum((v - st.mean(x)) ** 2 for v in x) / len(x). Change the denominator to len(x) - 1 for sample variance. ** 2 means “square.”

For nonnegative weights w with the same length as x and a positive total, the weighted mean is sum(v * weight for v, weight in zip(x, w)) / sum(w).
