01One outcome, several groupsFactors, levels, observations and the unit of analysis+

Why not run three separate t tests?

Class 6 compared two means. With three packing processes we could test A versus B, A versus C and B versus C, but repeated unadjusted tests create more opportunities for false alarms. Begin with one global question: are all population means equal? ANOVA answers that question before any planned, appropriately adjusted follow-up comparisons.

A fulfillment company tries three packing processes. For each independently sampled order, record the processing time in minutes and the process used. The response is numeric; the process is a categorical factor; A, B and C are its levels. One-way ANOVA asks whether their population mean times are all equal.

Long format

One row per order, with columns minutes and process. Keep the group label attached to the observation. This layout works well for filtering, charts and statistical models.

Wide format

One column per process, with observations beneath each heading. Groups can have different lengths. Blank padding is not a zero-minute order. Excel’s Single Factor tool can use this layout.

{{math:group-index}}

Count independent units, not just rows. Four repeated measurements on the same order are not four independent orders. Repeated customers, branches, shifts or batches may need a paired, repeated-measures or multilevel analysis. Random assignment supports a causal treatment comparison; ordinary grouping alone does not.

02Means plus unexplained variationThe normal model, treatment effects and the right grand mean+

Give each order a baseline and a deviation

Two orders using the same process still take different times. Think of a population process mean as the baseline and the error as the order’s departure from it. The model separates systematic group differences from that ordinary within-process variation. We observe sample means, not the true baselines, so even apparently separated means need an uncertainty comparison.

Each time equals its process’s population mean plus order-to-order noise. Classical fixed-effects ANOVA assumes independent errors, a common population variance and normal errors within groups. The factor levels are the specific levels of interest.

{{math:one-model}}{{math:one-means}}

For the example, the group means are 20, 24 and 27 minutes, and the grand sample mean is 23⅔ minutes. Equal group sizes let us average the three means directly. With unequal sizes, the grand sample mean must weight by the counts.

Technical depth · Two meaningful averages, two different targets

{{math:equal-target}}{{math:weighted-target}}

Both targets are valid, but they answer different questions. Equal-level effects are estimated by subtracting the average of group means. Weighted effects subtract the overall sample mean and obey a weighted zero-sum constraint. The usual SSG formula below uses the sample-size-weighted grand mean. A population business average could need other weights, such as the intended order mix; sample sizes do not automatically define that target.

03 / 14 · THE PACKING COMPANY

Split the total variation

{{anovavisual:split}}

Why can we add the squares only after summing?

Keep the original Process A orders: 18, 20, 22, 20. Their deviations from their own mean are −2, 0, +2, 0. The group mean is always 3⅔ minutes below the grand mean, so every order shares the same between-group piece, −11/3.

  1. One order does not work. For the 18-minute order, (−17/3)² = 289/9, but (−2)² + (−11/3)² = 157/9. The missing 132/9 is the cross-term: twice the product of the two pieces.
  2. The opposite departure cancels it. The 22-minute order has within deviation +2, so its cross-term is −132/9. The two 20-minute orders have zero within deviation and zero cross-term.
  3. The whole group does work. Total squares around the grand mean: 556/9. Within squares: 8. Between squares: 4 × (11/3)² = 484/9. Now 8 + 484/9 = 556/9.

Carry forward: Each group’s within deviations sum to zero. Its shared between-group piece therefore produces cross-terms that sum to zero. Repeating this for A, B and C gives SST = SSW + SSG. This is an arithmetic identity, before any probability assumptions enter.

Check: Would the identity work for only the 18-minute order?

No. Its cross-term has not canceled. Keep the complete groups and use their own sample means.

03Split the variation into signal and noiseTotal, between-group and within-group sums of squares+

Work one order before reading the sums

Take the 18-minute order in Process A. Relative to the grand mean 23⅔, its deviation is −5⅔. That can be split into −2 from its own group mean 20 and −3⅔ from the group mean to the grand mean. The first part is within-process noise; the second is between-process separation. Squared pieces add across the whole dataset after cross-terms cancel, not separately for every single order.

Imagine predicting every order with one number: the grand mean. Now improve the prediction by using the relevant process mean. The reduction in squared prediction error is the variation attributed to grouping.

{{math:one-ss}}

Total · SST

All variation around the grand mean. In this example, 122⅔ min².

Between · SSG

Variation explained by different sample group means, weighted by group counts. Here 98⅔ min².

Within · SSW

Residual variation around each group’s own mean. Each group contributes 8, giving 24 min².

The names differ between books and software: SSG may be called SSBetween or treatment SS; SSW may be called SSE or residual SS. Check the definition, not only the initials.

Technical depth · Why the pieces add

{{math:one-proof}}

Expand the square, sum, and cancel the cross term. This identity is algebraic; it does not require normality. Normality and independence become important when assigning the exact F reference distribution.

Pause and explain

A process mean moves farther from the grand mean while its within-group spread stays fixed. Which piece carries the signal?

Check your reasoning

The between-group component changes; the within-group residual spread need not change. This is why ANOVA contrasts separation of means with variation around those means, rather than comparing raw totals without a noise scale.

04 / 14 · THE PACKING COMPANY

Compare signal with noise

{{anovavisual:ratio}}

Why use variances to ask about means?

Even if all three population means are equal, new samples will have different means. The question is whether the observed separation is unusually large relative to ordinary order-to-order noise. First turn the two piles of squared minutes into comparable estimates.

  1. Count what was fitted. There are 12 independent orders, four per process. Within Process A, the deviations from its fitted mean are −2, 0, +2, 0 minutes. They must sum to zero: once three are known, the fourth is determined. Each of the three fitted group means uses one degree of freedom, leaving 12 − 3 = 9 within-group degrees of freedom.
  2. Turn within-group squares into a noise estimate. The three groups contribute 8 + 8 + 8 = 24 min². Divide by those 9 remaining degrees of freedom: MSW = 24/9 = 2⅔ min². A mean square is a sum of squared deviations divided by its degrees of freedom, not the average processing time.
  3. Put group separation on the same scale. The three group means vary around one fitted grand mean, leaving 3 − 1 = 2 between-group degrees of freedom. SSG already weights each group’s squared distance by its four orders. Divide 98⅔ min² by 2: MSG = 49⅓ min².

Now compare. Under the equal-means model, the between-group mean square and within-group mean square estimate the same common error variance. “Signal divided by noise” is shorthand: the numerator contains sampling noise too. Here 49⅓ min² ÷ 2⅔ min² = 18.5, with no units. This is evidence to compare with the F reference distribution, not “18.5 times faster.”

Random ratios vary even under equal means; 1 is a useful comparison point, not the rejection cutoff or the exact expected F value. Step 5 supplies the appropriate cutoff and tail probability.

Check: Why not divide the 24 min² by all 12 orders?

That would give 2 min² and ignore that three means were estimated from these same orders. Under the common-variance independent-error model, dividing by 9 corrects this loss of degrees of freedom when estimating the error variance. For the F ratio, use each component’s own degrees of freedom; do not divide both sums by 12.

See what a degree of freedom counts

Predict first: if we add independent orders to each existing group, does between-group df increase?

Residuals around their own sample mean sum to zero. Once the others are chosen, the final residual must balance their sum.

04Is the between-group difference unusually large?Degrees of freedom, mean squares and the upper-tail F test+

Why divide one kind of variation by another?

Even if all population means are equal, sample group means will differ. Between-group variation is therefore not automatically a treatment effect. Under equal means, the two mean squares estimate the same underlying noise variance; their ratio should not be systematically enormous. A large ratio says the observed group separation is hard to explain using the within-group noise alone.

{{math:one-null}}

Raw sums of squares grow with the number of observations and fitted effects. Divide each by its degrees of freedom to obtain a mean square. Total df is n−1; between-group df is c−1; within-group df is n−c.

{{math:one-f}}

For the example, MSG=49⅓ and MSW=2⅔, so F=18.5 with (2,9) df. The 5% upper-tail critical value is about 4.2565, and the p-value is 0.000648. Reject equal population mean times at α=.05, subject to the sampling and model assumptions.

{{math:one-p}}

Use the observed statistic in the p-value event. The tail beyond the critical value is α by construction, not the observed p-value. Here the quantile subscript is a lower-tail probability; an upper-tail F table may label the same cutoff with α instead.

Why only the upper tail? Under a mean difference, MSG tends to increase while MSW still estimates error variance. Squaring has already removed the direction. This does not restrict the alternative to means increasing.

Click it and compare the table: the means and SSG stay fixed, squared within-group deviations become four times as large, and F drops from 18.5 to 4.625. 05Where the F distribution comes fromIndependent chi-squares, expected mean squares and F = t²+

Connect a new distribution to a familiar test

The practical idea comes first: compare two estimates of noise, calibrated for how much independent information each contains. The technical derivation below explains the exact F reference under normal independent errors. If you skip the proof, keep its consequence: the reference distribution is a model-based guarantee, not a property of every dataset that can produce an ANOVA table.

Technical depth · The exact normal-model argument

{{math:chisquares}}

Within each group with nᵢ≥2, (nᵢ−1)sᵢ²/σ² has nᵢ−1 chi-square df. Add independent groups to obtain n−c. A singleton group contributes zero within-group SS and df, without defining its own sample variance. Under H₀, the standardized group means form a normal vector; projecting away the common-mean direction leaves c−1 orthogonal directions. This gives the between-group chi-square, including unequal sample sizes. Normal sample means are independent of within-group residuals, making the two sums of squares independent. A ratio of these independent chi-squares divided by their df is F.

{{math:expected-ms}}

These are statements about repeated-sample expectations. They do not mean every observed F is at least one. Also E(F) is not generally E(MSG)/E(MSW); under H₀ it equals (n−c)/(n−c−2) when n−c>2.

For two groups, classical one-way ANOVA gives exactly the same decision as the two-sided pooled equal-variance t test. It does not reproduce Welch’s unequal-variance test or a one-sided t test.

{{math:f-t}}

The F numerator has one df. Squaring merges both t tails into the F upper tail.

Keep the conclusion honest

{{anovavisual:report}}

One test is not a family of tests

Predict first: if every null is true, does running ten separate 5% tests still give a 5% chance of any false rejection?

On a narrow screen, swipe the diagram sideways, or focus it and use the left and right arrow keys. Its values are also explained below.

Observe: No false alarms has probability 0.953; at least one has probability 1−0.953 = 14.26%.

Interpret: This calculation assumes independent tests and all nulls true. Pairwise ANOVA comparisons share data, so this is an illustration, not their exact family risk. Bonferroni uses 0.05/3 ≈ 0.0167 per planned test to keep family false-rejection risk at most 5%, even with dependence. That protection can reduce power. Choose the comparison family before seeing results.

Check your explanation: Does a significant global F test identify a particular pair?

No. It supports that not all means are equal. Locating differences requires planned contrasts or appropriate multiplicity-adjusted comparisons.

Which differences survive the follow-up?

Use the original three packing processes, with sample means A = 20, B = 24 and C = 27 minutes and four independent orders per process. The global F test rejected equal means. Now suppose the analysis plan specified all three pairwise differences, B − A, C − A and C − B, as one comparison family before seeing outcomes. Positive values mean the first process takes longer.

  1. Reuse the within-process noise estimate. The common-variance model gives MSW = 24/9 = 8/3 min². Every difference has estimated SE = √[(8/3) × (1/4 + 1/4)] ≈ 1.1547 minutes, with 9 error degrees of freedom.
  2. Protect the whole family. Bonferroni allows 0.05/3 for each two-sided interval’s noncoverage, split across its two tails. The lower-tail t quantile is t9, 1−0.05/6 ≈ 2.9333, not the ordinary 95% multiplier. Multiply by the SE to get a margin of 3.3871 minutes.
  3. Keep the differences and units. Each interval is its estimated difference ± 3.3871 minutes. Under the independent normal, common-variance model, this procedure covers all three fixed population differences simultaneously in at least 95% of repeated datasets. The intervals share data; independence between comparisons is not required for Bonferroni’s bound.

On a narrow screen, scroll the plot sideways, or focus it and use the arrow keys. Exact rounded endpoints follow the plot.

  • B − A: 4 minutes, interval [0.6129, 7.3871].
  • C − A: 7 minutes, interval [3.6129, 10.3871].
  • C − B: 3 minutes, interval [−0.3871, 6.3871].

Decide before revealing: which processes are separated by these intervals? If a change must save at least 3 minutes per order to justify its cost, do the B − A results establish that much saving?

Read zero and the useful saving separately

The A comparisons exclude zero in the positive direction: these intervals provide evidence, under this model, that A has a lower population mean time than B and C. The C − B interval includes zero, so this family-adjusted comparison does not separate them; it does not show they are equivalent.

The estimated B-to-A saving is 4 minutes, but its interval extends down to about 0.61 minutes. It does not establish a saving of at least 3 minutes. That distinction matters if implementation costs require the larger saving. The C − A interval is entirely above 3 minutes under the same model, but neither comparison by itself proves a causal benefit or accounts for rollout costs and a changed order mix.

The positive direction was read from two-sided intervals, not chosen afterward to get a smaller p-value. Bonferroni is one transparent option; Tukey’s procedure is designed specifically for all pairwise mean comparisons under the common-variance model. These intervals use the original data, not the edited lab, and do not promise a particular result in new data.

06A small p-value is the start of the reportOmnibus inference, effect size and follow-up comparisons+

A global signal does not choose a winner

Return to the packing decision. F=18.5 provides evidence against all means being equal. It does not certify Process A as best or tell us which pairwise differences are precise enough to act on. Use the estimated means to describe the pattern, then ask targeted follow-up questions and compare potential time savings with implementation costs.

The F test is omnibus: rejection says at least one mean differs. It does not identify which process pairs differ, prove every mean is different, or establish that the quickest process will stay quickest in new data.

  • Show the estimates. Means 20, 24 and 27 minutes make the direction and business magnitude visible.
  • Plan comparisons. Use prespecified contrasts or a suitable multiple-comparison method such as Tukey’s procedure for pairwise comparisons under the common-variance model. Do not run many unadjusted t tests and ignore multiplicity.
  • Check usefulness. A seven-minute difference may matter, but consider implementation costs, uncertainty, order mix and study design.
  • Do not accept equality. A nonsignificant result means insufficient evidence to reject under the chosen design and α. It is not proof of equality or equivalence.
{{math:effect-size}}

Here η²≈.8043 and ω²≈.7447. η² describes this sample’s explained variation; ω² is a bias-reduced estimator under the model. Neither is a causal percentage. The lab displays the raw ω² estimate, which can be negative in weak-signal samples; some reporting conventions truncate it at zero. Both are undefined when all values are identical.

08Main effects plus a combination effectGrand, marginal and cell means; identifiable effects+

Understand interaction before interpreting averages

If the bundle added the same amount on both day types, one offer effect plus one day effect could describe the four means. Here it does not: the bundle gain changes from $4 to $12. The model therefore needs a combination term. Main effects average over this pattern; they are useful summaries only when their averaging matches the decision.

{{math:two-model}}

A main effect describes an average over the other factor’s levels. Interaction is the part of the cell mean not explained by simply adding the two main effects. It asks whether an effect depends on the other factor.

{{math:two-pop-means}}{{math:two-effects}}

Technical depth · Enough constraints to identify the model

{{math:two-constraints}}

A single grand sum of interaction effects equal to zero is insufficient. Row and column constraints leave (c−1)(r−1) independent interaction terms. Together, 1+(c−1)+(r−1)+(c−1)(r−1)=cr parameters describe the cr cell means.

Ask three distinct questions

{{anovavisual:tests}}

Put the change in the offer effect on a noise scale

The retailer needs to know whether the bundle’s advantage changes by day, not merely whether weekend spending is higher. Keep the four original cell means and three independent transactions per cell.

  1. Compare offers within each day. Weekday: $24 − $20 = $4. Weekend: $38 − $26 = $12. Comparing like day types keeps the ordinary weekend increase separate from the offer’s advantage.
  2. Compare those two advantages. $12 − $4 = $8. This difference of differences estimates how much larger the bundle advantage is on weekends. A zero population contrast means the two offer effects are equal, not that either effect must be zero.
  3. Allow for four estimated means. The within-cell MSE is 4 dollars². Each mean, based on three independent transactions, has estimated variance 4/3. Under the common-variance independent-error model, the variance estimates add even when the means are subtracted: SE = √(4/3 + 4/3 + 4/3 + 4/3) ≈ $2.31.

Connect to the table: 8 ÷ √(16/3) ≈ 3.464 is a t statistic with 8 residual df under the original normal-error model. Squaring gives 12, exactly the interaction F(1,8). This equivalence uses this two-by-two design’s single interaction contrast; a larger design can have several interaction directions.

Predict, then check: If within-cell standard deviation doubles but cell means stay fixed, what happens?

The $4 and $12 gains and their $8 difference stay fixed. MSE quadruples to 16, the contrast SE doubles to about $4.62, and F falls from 12 to 3. The apparent pattern is the same but the evidence is weaker. Neither test alone establishes that the bundle is profitable or that the association is causal.

11Three questions, three F testsMain effects averaged over levels, and the interaction null+

Read the interaction result before giving a blanket recommendation

There are three distinct questions: do average offers differ, do average day types differ, and does the offer effect change across days? Plan which questions matter before testing. Interpret interaction first so that an average main effect is not presented as a universal effect. A nonsignificant interaction alone also does not prove the effect is constant; consider precision and the cell pattern.

{{math:two-tests}}

Each alternative is “at least one of the tested effects is nonzero.” These are separate upper-tail tests. For the example, F values are 48, 75 and 12; all use (1,8) df, with 5% critical value ≈5.3177. The p-values are about .000121, .0000246 and .008516.

All three tests reject at nominal 5%. The interaction says the offer effect is not constant across day types. The A main effect remains an average contrast over the two B levels. It does not claim the same $8 benefit in every context. Three nominal 5% tests do not jointly guarantee a 5% familywise error rate; plan primary questions and any multiplicity adjustments.

Make the hidden pattern visible

An interaction plot is a comparison of comparisons. Follow each offer across day types, then compare the vertical gaps. Equal gaps produce parallel lines; different gaps produce nonparallel lines. Lines do not need to cross for interaction to exist. Crossing is an especially strong warning that a single average ranking may be misleading.

Plot the cell means, not only the marginal means. In the default example, the bundle-minus-plain difference is $4 on weekdays and $12 on weekends. The difference of differences is $8. That is the business meaning of interaction.

Try parallel: a constant offer effect produces zero sample interaction. Try crossover: the better offer reverses between day types. Both marginal offer means can then be equal even when the cell-level differences are large. Nonparallel sample lines suggest interaction; the F test assesses that departure relative to within-cell noise.

Predict → change → observe → explain: Do not let the average hide the recommendation

Predict what the interaction and offer main effect could do in a crossover. Try the crossover preset, read both within-day offer differences, then inspect the marginal means and F tests. Explain how opposite offer gains can cancel in the average while interaction remains. Reset and try parallel effects: equal within-day gains remove sample interaction even though an overall offer difference can remain. Sample parallelism is not proof that population interaction is exactly zero.

Use simple effects when interaction matters. Report offer differences within each day type with suitable confidence intervals and multiplicity control. Main effects alone may be a poor decision summary, even when mathematically valid. A small interaction p-value does not by itself establish a useful strategy or a causal mechanism.

13Check the experiment before trusting the outputIndependence, residuals, variance, balance and alternatives+

Ask whether the denominator is credible

Every F ratio relies on its error mean square as a meaningful estimate of noise. Duplicated customers, changing spread or missing combinations can break that interpretation. Diagnose the sampling units and residuals before trusting a precise p-value. No amount of clean arithmetic repairs an experiment that counted dependent rows as independent replications.

  • Independence comes from design. Check sampling, randomization, repeated units and clustering. A residual plot cannot prove independence; inspect time or run order when relevant.
  • Normal errors, not a normal mixture. Look at residuals around group or cell means using a Q–Q plot and outlier checks. The combined response distribution may be nonnormal simply because means differ.
  • Common variance. Compare within-group or within-cell spread and residual-versus-fitted plots. Unequal sizes plus unequal variances can be especially problematic. No single diagnostic p-value certifies the model.
  • Extreme observations. Verify measurement or entry errors rather than deleting inconvenient observations. If genuinely extreme values remain, assess sensitivity and a method suited to the outcome.
  • Balanced replicated two-way design. The simple SS formulas need complete cells with the same replicate count. Unequal replication or missing cells calls for a general linear model with a stated estimand and appropriate sums of squares.

For one-way unequal variances, consider Welch ANOVA and compatible follow-up comparisons. A rank-based method such as Kruskal–Wallis addresses a different distributional question; it is not automatically a test of means. For correlated observations, use an appropriate repeated-measures or mixed model. Robust and randomization methods require their own assumptions.

Zero residual variation is a warning. If different group/cell means have no within-cell spread, the formal ratio is infinite; if numerator and denominator are both zero, it is undefined. The labs label these cases rather than silently returning a routine finite test. Check whether the data are synthetic, rounded, duplicated or too sparse for realistic uncertainty.

Written practice and solutions
  1. What does rejecting one-way ANOVA establish? At least one population mean differs, subject to the model. It does not identify every differing pair.
  2. Unequal sample sizes: how is the grand sample mean calculated? Weight group means by their sample sizes. The equal average of group means estimates a different target.
  3. Which tail is used for the mean-comparison F test? The upper tail. Squared deviations detect separation in any direction.
  4. What goes inside the p-value event? The observed F statistic, not the critical value.
  5. Why n−c residual df? Each of c fitted group means uses one degree of freedom, leaving n−c residual directions.
  6. How is ANOVA related to a pooled two-sample t test? For two groups, F=t² and the upper-tail F p-value equals the two-sided pooled t p-value.
  7. What is the error term in replicated two-way ANOVA? Within-cell deviations from the corresponding cell mean.
  8. What constraints identify interaction effects? Each interaction row and column sums to zero. A single grand zero-sum constraint is insufficient.
  9. Can large interaction coexist with no average offer effect? Yes. A crossover can cancel in the marginal means.
  10. One observation per cell in a full interaction model? No residual degrees of freedom; interaction cannot be separated from noise using this design alone.
  11. What does a nonsignificant result prove? It does not prove equality. Evaluate precision, power, design and practical importance.
  12. What happens if you test many pairs at unadjusted 5%? The chance of at least one false positive can rise. Plan contrasts or control multiple comparisons.

Editorial corrections

The overall sample mean is sample-size weighted; an equal average of population group means is a distinct estimand when groups are unequal. Exact tests require independent errors. Interactions need row and column zero-sum constraints, not just a grand sum.

p-values use the observed F statistic. MSE is within-cell error, not between-group variation. Worked arithmetic, subscripts and cell-centering mistakes are corrected. Non-rejection is not acceptance, and a significant main effect need not describe every cell when interaction is present.
