START HERE · YOUR LEARNING PATH

Would you plan a budget around $72?

A retailer samples orders and finds a mean value of $72. A manager wants to use that number in next quarter’s budget. Before committing, ask how much sampling noise could move it and whether the resulting range is precise enough for the decision. This class turns one estimate into a defensible statement of uncertainty.

Bring from Class 4: a statistic changes across samples; its sampling distribution describes that variation; SD describes individuals while SE describes the statistic. Review standard error and Student t if needed. We will now use those ideas when the population target is unknown.

  1. Choose and evaluate a rule (1–3): distinguish the parameter, estimator and observed estimate, then compare bias, precision and consistency.
  2. Estimate uncertainty (4–5): build an estimated SE for amounts or binary outcomes.
  3. Build and interpret a range (6–9): understand estimate ± margin, repeated coverage, t intervals and proportion intervals.
  4. Plan and communicate (10–12): choose a useful margin before gathering data, reproduce a result and explain what a manager can conclude.

Read the numbered sections in order. Keep a short decision note with four headings: target, estimate, uncertainty, limitations. Predict each lab change before running it. Technical depth adds proofs; the main reading contains the reasoning needed to use the methods.

Name the target

{{estvisual:target}}

01One target. A rule. A result.Population parameter, estimator, estimate and interval+

Reverse the direction of the question

In Class 4 we supplied a population mean and asked how sample means vary around it. The retailer does not know that population mean. We now observe a sample and work back toward the target. Keep three things separate: what we want to know, the rule we will use to learn it, and the number that rule produces today. The five-order example is a small arithmetic warm-up; the later 81-order example supports the budget discussion.

A retailer wants the mean value of all eligible orders this quarter. That unknown population mean is the target. A randomly selected set of orders gives a way to learn about it.

Parameter θ

A fixed, usually unknown feature of a specified population: mean μ, variance σ², or proportion π.

Estimator θ̂

A rule applied to a random sample. Before collecting data, its value is random because a different sample could give a different answer.

Estimate

The realized result after applying the rule to the observed data. It is a number, not a new random draw.

{{math:estimate}}

Five synthetic order values, $46, $52, $48, $54 and $50, give the point estimate $50 using the sample mean. An interval estimator produces two random endpoints; the realized interval estimates a range of plausible population values using a stated method.

A manager’s forecast range is not automatically a confidence interval. A confidence interval needs a target, sampling model, method and confidence level. Nor is a confidence interval for a mean a prediction interval for the next order.

Compare the rules

{{estvisual:quality}}

Ready for the detail? Open the original explanations below. All formulas, proofs, examples and exercises are retained.

02Good on average is not the whole storyUnbiasedness, efficiency, consistency and mean squared error+

Why not trust any rule that returns a number?

Two analysts can apply different rules to the same orders. To compare them fairly, imagine repeating the collection many times under the same model. Does a rule systematically miss the target? How widely do its answers vary? Does more data solve the problem? These are different questions, called bias, precision and consistency. A tightly clustered set of wrong answers is not a success.

Assume a representative IID sample with a finite variance. Three properties ask different questions about an estimator across repeated samples.

{{math:bias}}

Unbiasedness: is the rule centered on the target? It does not promise that any particular estimate is correct. Efficiency: among unbiased estimators of the same target under the same model, lower variance means greater precision. “Most efficient” is meaningful only within a specified comparison class.

{{math:mse}}

MSE lets us compare biased and unbiased rules on squared error. It does not tell us whether a business loss is actually symmetric or quadratic.

{{math:consistency}}

Consistency: does the rule concentrate near the true target as n grows? This is convergence in probability, not a promise of exact equality, monotone improvement, or almost-sure convergence. A rule can be unbiased but inconsistent: using only X₁ ignores every additional observation.

Try it · Precision can hide a persistent mistake

  1. Predict: when n grows, will “sample mean + 2” eventually center on the true mean of 50?
  2. Change: compare n=25 with n=100 for “sample mean + 2.” Then switch to “sample mean + 12/n.”
  3. Observe: compare the centers, spreads, bias and MSE rather than judging only the narrowness of the curves.
  4. Explain: which rule’s bias shrinks with n, and why does that matter for consistency?
Check your explanation

Both shifted rules have variance 144/n. The fixed +2 rule narrows around 52, keeping bias 2, so its MSE approaches 4 rather than 0. The +12/n rule has bias .48 at n=25 and .12 at n=100; both its bias and variance tend to zero. More data addresses sampling noise, not a permanent two-unit error.

Technical depth

Here the population mean is 50 and SD is 12. The green mean has variance 144/n. The orange shifted mean has the same variance, but either constant bias 2 or shrinking bias 12/n. Increasing n cannot remove constant bias.

{{math:consistency-sufficient}}

Vanishing variance alone is not enough: a constant rule returning 51 has zero variance and, when the true θ=50, always misses the target by 1. The same warning applies to a concentrated but systematically biased data source. Conversely, consistency alone does not guarantee unbiasedness at finite n.

03Why the simple average earns its placeUnbiasedness, efficiency among linear rules, and consistency+

Choose a rule that uses the information

One possible rule is to use the first order and ignore the rest. It is centered correctly under IID sampling, but buying more data would never improve it. The ordinary mean gives every sampled order a role, allowing independent positive and negative deviations to offset one another. This explains why its uncertainty shrinks with sample size. The formula below connects that choice to the sampling distribution from Class 4.

{{math:sample-mean}}

Equal weights average independent noise. The standard error is σ/√n. Quadrupling the sample size halves it, assuming the same sampling model. The law of large numbers supplies consistency.

Technical depth

Consider deterministic weights wᵢ that sum to 1. For independent observations with the same mean and variance, every such weighted mean is unbiased. Independence removes covariance terms.

{{math:weighted-mean}}{{math:cauchy}}

This is an efficiency statement about fixed linear unbiased estimators under common variance and independence. It does not say that equal weighting is optimal for unequal variances, clustered data, survey weights, or every possible estimator.

Estimate the spread

{{estvisual:spread}}

Ready for the detail? Open the original explanation below. All formulas, proofs, examples and exercises are retained.

04Why variance divides by n minus oneUnbiased sample variance, proof and degrees of freedom+

We also need to learn how noisy orders are

A point estimate alone cannot tell the retailer whether $72 is stable. Usually the population spread is unknown too, so we estimate it from deviations within the sample. Measuring deviations around the sample’s own fitted mean makes them a little too small on average: that mean was chosen from these very observations. Dividing their squared sum by n−1 corrects this bias for population variance under IID sampling. It does not mean that one order has been discarded.

{{math:sample-variance}}

Our five order values have mean 50 and squared deviations 16, 4, 4, 16 and 0. Their sum is 40. Dividing by 4 gives s²=10 dollars²; dividing by 5 gives 8 dollars². The latter is the descriptive variance of these five values, but is a downward-biased estimator of the wider population variance.

The residuals must sum to zero. Once n−1 residuals are known, the last is determined. Estimating the mean uses one degree of freedom. This is an explanation of the constraint, not a claim that the remaining residuals are independent.

Technical depth

{{math:variance-identity}}{{math:variance-proof}}{{math:second-moment}}
{{math:estimated-se}}

For the five orders, s=√10≈3.1623 and estimated SE=√10/√5≈1.4142 dollars. Do not confuse spread of orders with uncertainty in their average. S² is unbiased; taking a square root does not make S or S/√n unbiased in general.

Count conversions

{{estvisual:binary}}

Ready for the detail? Open the original explanation below. All formulas, proofs, examples and exercises are retained.

05A conversion rate is a sample meanBinary variance and plug-in standard error+

Carry the same idea from dollars to conversions

The retailer may also budget from the conversion rate. Coding each eligible visitor as 0 or 1 makes that rate a mean, just as in Class 4. The difference now is that the true conversion probability π is unknown. A plug-in estimate substitutes the observed rate for π in the variance formula. That is a useful approximation, but it can fail when a small sample has no conversions.

Code a conversion as 1 and no conversion as 0. With independent, constant-probability Bernoulli trials, population mean π is the conversion proportion. The sample mean p is conversions divided by trials.

{{math:binary-mean}}{{math:binary-variance}}{{math:binary-se}}

For 48 conversions out of 200, p=.24; plug-in individual variance is .1824, unbiased individual variance is about .1833166, and the usual plug-in SE of p is .0301993, or 3.02 percentage points. The conventional Wald interval uses the plug-in SE. Do not silently replace one formula with another or call either square-root SE unbiased.

Technical depth

Since E(p²)=Var(p)+[E(p)]², E[p(1−p)]=π−π²−π(1−π)/n. This gives the downward-bias factor (n−1)/n. When all observed outcomes are equal, the plug-in SE is zero; it does not establish a certain population rate.

Build an interval

{{estvisual:interval}}

Ready for the detail? Open the original explanation below. All formulas, proofs, examples and exercises are retained.

06Build a range around the averageKnown population variance, normal model and z interval+

An interval is an estimate plus an error allowance

Imagine repeatedly estimating the retailer’s mean order value. Most sample means lie within a few SEs of the true mean under the model. We use that behavior to build a range around the observed mean: start at the estimate, then go a chosen number of SEs in either direction. The multiplier is a critical value; multiplier × SE is the margin of error. For a 95% normal interval, about 2.5% of the reference distribution is left in each tail.

A retailer’s process model supplies a known population SD of $18. A random sample of 81 orders has mean $72. Under IID normal sampling, a 95% z interval is exact. Under suitable finite-variance IID conditions, a sufficiently accurate CLT approximation can justify it approximately for a nonnormal population. There is no universal sample-size cutoff.

{{math:z-pivot}}{{math:z-ci}}

For 95% confidence, α=.05 and z.025≈1.959964. Here SE=18/9=2, ME≈3.9199 and the interval is [$68.0801, $75.9199]. The lower and upper confidence limits are the point estimate minus and plus ME.

Technical depth

{{math:z-proof}}

Equivalently, choose m so that P(μ−m≤X̄≤μ+m)=1−α. Standardization gives m/(σ/√n)=zα/2, so m=ME. For a CLT approximation, replace the equality of coverage with an approximation.

06 / 10 · THE UNCERTAINTY

Read 95% correctly

{{estvisual:coverage}}

The average budget and the next order need different ranges

The retailer's 81-order sample has mean $72 and known population SD $18. Its 95% confidence interval, $68.08–$75.92, concerns the population mean. A manager packing the next order needs a different answer: one order still varies on the scale of $18, even when the average is estimated precisely.

Predict: would collecting four times as many orders make a range for the next individual order half as wide, as it does for the mean's confidence interval?

Separate uncertainty in the mean from variation in the next order

No. For a new order independent of the sample, its difference from the sample mean contains two sources of variation. Under the same fixed IID normal model with known σ=$18, their variances add: 18² + 18²/81 = 328 dollars². The prediction standard deviation is √328 ≈ $18.11, rather than the mean's SE of $2.

A 95% normal prediction interval is therefore 72 ± 1.959964 × √328, approximately $36.50–$107.50. This procedure covers the future order in 95% of joint repetitions of the sample and new order under that model. It is not a confidence interval for the population mean.

At n=324, the mean's SE halves to $1, but the prediction standard deviation only falls to √325 ≈ $18.03. Learning the mean more precisely cannot eliminate differences between orders. This exact prediction formula requires normal individual outcomes and known σ; a CLT for the sample mean alone does not justify it for skewed order values, and a changing next-quarter process needs further modeling.

0795% describes the procedureRepeated-sampling coverage, not probability of a fixed mean+

Why call that range 95% confident?

The interval is useful only if its reliability has a clear meaning. Think of a procedure the retailer could repeat: select a fresh sample, calculate its mean, calculate its margin and report the endpoints. Different samples produce different intervals around the same fixed population mean. The 95% label describes how often that whole procedure covers the target under its assumptions. The simulation makes this visible because, unlike a real business study, we know the true mean.

Before the sample is drawn, the interval endpoints are random. In repeated use under the stated assumptions, a 95% method covers the fixed population mean in 95% of repetitions. Once an interval has been computed, it either covers that mean or it does not.

Say this

“Using this sampling method and model, our 95% confidence interval for the population mean is 68.08 to 75.92.”

Do not infer this

“95% of orders fall inside the interval,” “the next order has a 95% chance of falling here,” or “there is a 95% frequentist probability that the fixed μ is inside this realized interval.”

Try it · Watch the procedure, not just one interval

  1. Predict: should every interval cover 50 at 95% confidence? Will n=100 produce wider or narrower intervals than n=25?
  2. Change: use z, 95% confidence and B=200; compare n=25 with n=100. Then keep n=25 and change confidence to 99%.
  3. Observe: compare widths and coverage counts. Try another random seed. Finally compare B=20 and B=500 with the other settings fixed.
  4. Explain: distinguish changing an interval’s precision, changing its nominal coverage and stabilizing the simulated coverage estimate.
Check your explanation

At 95%, some intervals are expected to miss the fixed mean. Quadrupling n halves the z margin but leaves the method’s nominal coverage at 95%. Raising confidence to 99% widens the intervals. Changing B changes the number of experiments, not the theoretical width or nominal coverage of one interval. A finite run can be above or below its nominal percentage, even when the method is correctly calibrated.

Use an estimated SD

{{estvisual:student}}

Ready for the detail? Open the original explanation below. All formulas, proofs, examples and exercises are retained.

08When the population SD is unknownStudent t, degrees of freedom and estimated standard error+

Replace the idealized known spread with an estimated one

The known-SD example isolated the logic of an interval. Now remove its strongest convenience: most retailers do not know σ. Using s gives an estimated SE, but the SD estimate can itself be too small or too large. Student t compensates with a larger critical value under the normal model. The structure remains estimate ± margin; what changes is how we account for uncertainty in the estimated spread.

For a typical business sample, σ is not actually known. Replace it with the sample SD s and use a t critical value. For IID normal observations this gives an exact finite-sample confidence interval; for nonnormal finite-variance IID data, it is generally only an asymptotic approximation. Small samples with strong skewness or outliers call for careful modeling.

{{math:t-pivot}}

Sixteen independently sampled repair times have mean 47 minutes and s=8 minutes. With df=15, the 95% t critical value is 2.13145. Estimated SE=2, ME≈4.2629 and the interval is [42.7371, 51.2629] minutes. The exact calculation assumes a normal population model.

Try it · Would the repair-time evidence justify the claim?

  1. Predict: with mean 47, s=8 and n=16, will a 95% interval exclude a benchmark of 50 minutes?
  2. Change: calculate the default t interval. Compare it with z using the same numeric SD input, then return to t and raise confidence to 99%.
  3. Observe: keep the center separate from the critical value and margin.
  4. Explain: why is choosing the narrower z result not justified merely because it looks more decisive?
Check your explanation

The 95% t interval is about 42.74–51.26, so 50 is inside it. z gives a smaller margin for the same numeric inputs, but that comparison does not turn an estimated SD into a known population SD. At 99% the t interval is wider. The sample suggests a lower mean, but this 95% two-sided interval does not exclude 50; that is different from proving equality or proving there is no useful improvement.

Choose the right interval

{{mean-chart-default}}
{{mean-metrics-default}}

{{mean-note-default}}

For the same n, SD input and confidence level, t is wider than z because estimating σ adds uncertainty. Its critical value approaches z as df grows. A large historical dataset may supply a planning SD estimate; that does not make the population SD literally known.

Technical depth

For normal samples, (n−1)S²/σ² is chi-square with n−1 degrees of freedom and is independent of X̄. Dividing the standard normal mean pivot by the square root of that independent chi-square variable divided by its df yields Student t. See Class 4’s derivation.

Check a rate interval

{{estvisual:proportion}}

09A percentage needs an uncertainty rangeWald interval, count checks and a Wilson comparison+

A percentage also needs a margin in the right units

The observed 24% conversion rate is a point estimate, not a promise for the whole visitor population. Start with its estimated SE of about 3.02 percentage points. Multiplying by the 95% normal critical value gives an allowance of about 5.92 percentage points on each side. This simple interval is called Wald. Its simplicity is useful for understanding the construction, but the next lab shows why it is not safe in every sample.

{{math:wald}}

For 48 conversions out of 200, the 95% Wald interval is [.18081, .29919], or 18.08% to 29.92%. Its margin is about 5.92 percentage points, not a 5.92% relative change.

Check observed successes np and failures n(1−p). The source uses at least 5 each; a common, more cautious classroom screen is at least 10. Neither cutoff guarantees accurate coverage. The normal Wald interval can perform poorly near 0 or 1, collapse at zero/all successes, or extend beyond [0,1]. We show its raw result instead of silently clipping it.

Try it · Zero observed does not mean impossible

  1. Predict: if 0 of 200 visitors convert, is the true population conversion rate certainly zero?
  2. Change: inspect the default 48/200 interval, then set observed conversions to 0 while retaining n=200 and 95% confidence.
  3. Observe: compare the raw Wald interval with Wilson and read the count warning.
  4. Explain: why does a zero plug-in SE signal a weakness of this approximation rather than perfect knowledge?
Check your explanation

No conversions can occur even when the true conversion probability is positive. At p=0, the Wald plug-in formula uses p(1−p)=0, making its interval collapse to [0,0]. Wilson retains a positive upper bound (about 1.88% here), reflecting uncertainty the simple Wald calculation loses. This does not make Wilson universally exact; the sampling model still matters.

{{math:wilson}}

Wilson inverts the normal score inequality |p−π|≤z√[π(1−π)/n], retaining π in the variance while solving for its allowed values. The interval need not be symmetric about the observed p. Its addition here explains why the simple source formula needs a boundary warning.

Plan the precision

{{estvisual:planning}}

10Decide the precision before collecting dataSample size for a mean, rounding up and planning assumptions+

Let the business decision set the precision target

The retailer’s $72 estimate has a margin near $3.92, but suppose the budget requires accuracy within about $3. Rather than collect an arbitrary number of orders, choose the desired confidence and margin first. Then work backward from margin = critical value × SD / √n. Solving for n gives the formula below. A smaller acceptable error requires more observations, and fractions of observations must be rounded upward.

{{math:plan-mean}}

To estimate mean order value within ±$3 at 95% confidence with planning σ=$18, calculate 138.2925 and round up to 139 observations. The resulting margin is about $2.9924; 138 would be too few.

If σ is estimated from a pilot, the calculation is a planning approximation, not a guaranteed final width. A t-based design may require iteration because its critical value depends on n. Build in practical allowances for nonresponse and unusable records separately.

This formula targets a confidence interval for a population mean under the stated design. It does not establish representativeness, power for a hypothesis test, precision for every subgroup, or the size needed for a prediction interval.

11Why survey plans often use one halfConservative sample size, precision trade-offs and the 1,000 rule+

Plan a rate when you do not yet know the rate

For conversions, the planning spread depends on the unknown π as well as the sample size. If credible past data suggest 24%, use that as a labeled planning assumption. If no credible guess exists, ask which probability produces the most uncertain 0/1 outcomes: .5, where successes and failures are equally likely. Planning for that variance avoids understating the required n within the normal approximation.

{{math:plan-proportion}}

The true π is unknown: use a defensible planning guess p₀, perhaps from historical evidence. With p₀=.24, 95% confidence and target e=.04 (four percentage points), the normal planning formula gives 438 completed observations. If the guess is wrong, the actual precision can differ.

Without a defensible guess, use p₀=.5. It maximizes Bernoulli variance, giving 601 observations for the same target. This is conservative for the variance in this approximation, not a universal finite-sample coverage guarantee.

Try it · Price the precision before collecting data

  1. Predict: will halving a mean’s target margin from $3 to $1.50 require twice or roughly four times as many orders?
  2. Change: keep planning SD 18 and confidence 95%; compare margins 3 and 1.5. Then load “±4 percentage points” and compare planning proportions .5 and .24.
  3. Observe: compare required sample sizes and achieved margins; notice the upward rounding.
  4. Explain: write a recommendation that separates a useful planning guess from a guarantee.
Check your explanation

The mean plan rises from 139 to 554 orders, roughly fourfold before rounding. With a four-percentage-point target, .5 calls for 601 completed observations and .24 for 438. The smaller plan relies on the .24 assumption. Nonresponse, dependence or an inaccurate planning SD can change the precision achieved, so the collection plan must address those separately.

Apply the whole lesson · transfer tasks and ten-question practice

Give the manager a range and a reason

You started with a random variable for one order, used its sampling distribution to describe an average, and turned that sampling uncertainty into an interval for an unknown population parameter. An interval’s width tells the manager how precise the estimate is; its coverage describes the method’s repeated reliability. Neither replaces representative data or a judgment about the cost of being wrong.

Transfer task · Would you commit the budget?

The budget works only if mean order value exceeds $70. The retailer’s known-SD 95% interval is $68.08–$75.92 around $72. Write two sentences explaining what the sample supports and what you would check next.

Compare with a reasoned recommendation

“The point estimate is $72, but our 95% interval includes population means below the $70 requirement as well as above it. I would not treat the estimate as assurance that the requirement is met; I would check the sampling design and decide whether the potential loss justifies collecting a more precise sample.” More data can narrow sampling uncertainty, but a confidence interval is not a full decision rule and does not measure the probability that this budget will succeed.

Transfer task · What would change your conclusion?

A colleague claims that 99% confidence makes the estimate more precise and that 1,000 simulated intervals are equivalent to 1,000 additional orders. Correct both statements.

Check the distinctions

With the same data and method, 99% confidence makes the interval wider, trading precision for higher nominal coverage. Simulation repetitions help illustrate a procedure’s behavior; they do not add observations to the retailer’s actual sample. To reduce the margin at the same confidence level, collect more suitable independent observations or improve the design.

Next: turn a benchmark into a testable claim

Class 6 asks whether observed data conflict with a specified population value. In the repair example, 50 lies inside the 95% two-sided t interval, matching nonrejection in the corresponding two-sided 5% t test under the same model. That connection does not prove the benchmark true, and a one-sided business claim needs its own prespecified test. Bring forward the target, SE, assumptions and distinction between statistical evidence and practical importance.

Written practice and solutions
  1. The sample mean is an estimator before sampling; $50 calculated from five orders is an estimate.
  2. Unbiased means centered on the target over repeated samples, not exactly correct every time.
  3. Small variance alone does not imply consistency. The bias must also be controlled.
  4. Dividing squared residuals by n−1 corrects the bias from estimating the mean under IID sampling.
  5. 95% confidence describes repeated coverage of the procedure; it is not a probability assigned to the fixed parameter after the interval is computed.
  6. Higher confidence produces a wider interval when the other inputs are unchanged.
  7. Unknown σ with 16 IID normal observations uses t with 15 degrees of freedom.
  8. Zero conversions do not establish that the population conversion probability is zero; a zero-width Wald interval is misleading.
  9. Halving the target margin requires about four times as many observations, before rounding.
  10. Using p₀=.5 maximizes planning variance and gives a conservative normal-approximation sample size.

Editorial corrections

Consistency is convergence in probability, not almost-sure convergence. Vanishing variance alone is insufficient without controlling bias. Mean efficiency is scoped to fixed linear unbiased rules under equal variances and independence. Binary plug-in variance is separated from its unbiased correction, and the SD is not called unbiased.

Exact normal/t results are distinguished from large-sample approximations; confidence is interpreted through repeated sampling. Wald boundary failures are shown explicitly. Planning values use unrounded critical values internally and round sample sizes upward. The source’s reciprocal-square mnemonics are corrected.
