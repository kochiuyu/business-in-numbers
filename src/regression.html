01From groups to a continuous relationshipPredict a numeric outcome without confusing association with causation+

One decision, three different questions

The retailer needs to budget advertising and plan order-handling capacity. It asks: how does average demand vary with spend, how certain is that relationship, and what might one future week bring? These are a fitted relationship, coefficient inference and prediction. Keep them separate throughout the lesson. Unlike ANOVA’s process labels, advertising has numeric distances that a line can use.

A retailer wants to understand how advertising spend relates to weekly orders. In Class 7, ANOVA compared categories. Now the predictor is numeric: x = advertising spend, measured in $100 units. The response is y = weekly orders, measured in tens. Regression uses the size of x, not just its label.

Eight fictional market-week observations keep the arithmetic transparent. They are independently sampled for this teaching model. In a real dataset, repeated weeks in the same market can be correlated and require a different analysis. All data and diagrams here are newly created examples, not evidence about a real business.

Regress y on x means use x to explain or predict y. Reversing the roles generally gives a different fitted line, not the inverse of the first. “Independent variable” is a role name; it does not mean x and y are statistically independent.

A slope is not a causal effect by itself.

A busy market may both advertise more and receive more orders. Seasonality, promotions and market size can affect both variables. Random assignment of advertising can support a causal interpretation when the design and its assumptions are sound. Observational regression alone cannot establish one.

Read the slope in business units

{{regvisual:line}}

Ready for the calculations? Open the original explanation below. Keep the assumptions with the result.

02A straight line, with units attachedIntercept, slope, fitted values and useful limits+

Predict an average, not every observation

Imagine many comparable weeks at the same advertising spend. Their orders would still differ because of influences outside our model. A population line describes their mean; its unobserved departures are errors. A fitted line estimates that mean from the sample, and its observed departures are residuals. We start with the line’s meaning before calculating its coefficients.

{{math:line}}

Intercept · a

The fitted mean response when x=0. Here it is about 8.8214 tens of orders. But zero advertising is outside our observed range 1–8, so this baseline is an extrapolation, not a directly observed mean.

Slope · b

The fitted mean changes by 1.9286 tens of orders per $100 increase in advertising, about 19.29 orders per $100. This is a within-model association, not a guaranteed return on spending.

The line approximates the conditional mean of y at each x. Individual observations need not fall on it. Interpolation stays within the observed predictor range; extrapolation goes beyond it. Even a narrow model-based interval does not protect against a relationship changing outside that range.

03 / 15 · THE RETAILER

Compare two prediction rules

{{regvisual:fit}}

Ready for the calculations? Open the original explanation below. Keep the assumptions with the result.

03Fit the line by minimizing squared missesWhy vertical residuals, why squares, and a line you can move+

Make “best fitting” an explicit rule

Many lines slope upward through this cloud. Which one should we use? For each candidate, predict orders at every observed spend and record actual minus predicted orders. Adding signed misses can hide a poor fit through cancellation. Squared misses produce a score that cannot cancel and penalizes large errors; minimizing that score defines our fitted line.

{{math:objective}}

We measure vertical misses because we predict y given x, using y’s units. Positive and negative misses must not cancel. Squaring penalizes large misses strongly and gives a smooth optimization problem with a closed-form solution. It is a modeling choice, not the only possible loss.

Least absolute deviations minimizes absolute vertical misses and estimates a conditional median under its usual formulation. Orthogonal distance regression accounts for distances in both coordinates, often when both are measured with error; their scaling and measurement-error assumptions matter. Ordinary least squares can be sensitive to unusual observations.

Predict → change → observe → explain: A plausible line is not necessarily the best one

Before moving a slider, predict what a negative slope would do to the misses in this upward-trending cloud. Try it, then lower the sum of squares by adjusting slope and intercept. Use “Show least-squares line” and compare your score with the minimum. Explain why the optimum need not pass through any observed point: it compromises over all eight squared vertical misses.

04 / 15 · THE RETAILER

Find the center and the tilt

{{regvisual:center}}

Ready for the calculations? Open the original explanation below. Keep the assumptions with the result.

04Find the center, then estimate the tiltThe exact formulas, a worked example and normal equations+

Turn the visual search into a calculation

The lab searches over lines; the formulas find the minimizing line directly. Centering each variable measures departures from its own average. Sxy records whether those departures move together; Sxx records how much predictor variation is available. Their ratio converts joint movement into response change per unit of x. The intercept then places the line through the sample’s center.

{{math:centered-sums}}

For our eight observations, x̄=4.5, ȳ=17.5, Sxx=42, and Sxy=81. Therefore b=81/42=1.928571 and a=17.5−1.928571×4.5=8.821429. The fitted line passes through (4.5,17.5).

At x=3, the prediction is 14.607143 tens of orders. The observed 16 gives residual +1.392857: the line underpredicts. At x=4, the fitted 16.535714 exceeds the observed 15, giving residual −1.535714.

Technical depth · The minimizing equations

{{math:normal-equations}}{{math:coefficient-proof}}

When Sxx>0, the quadratic objective is strictly convex in the two coefficients, so this solution is the unique minimum. If every x is identical, no unique slope is identified. Fitting a line needs variation in x; estimating error variance and ordinary inference also needs n>2.

05 / 15 · THE RETAILER

Change units, not the forecast

{{regvisual:units}}

Ready for the calculations? Open the original explanation below. Keep the assumptions with the result.

05Change the units, not the relationshipCovariance, correlation, centering and rescaling+

A numerical slope needs a measurement scale

A slope of 1.9286 is meaningless without “tens of orders per $100.” Changing dollars to another unit changes the printed coefficient but should not change the predictions for the same spending amounts. Use rescaling as a sanity check: the business relationship must survive a harmless relabeling of units.

{{math:slope-covariance}}

Positive covariance gives a positive slope; negative covariance gives a negative slope. Zero covariance gives a horizontal least-squares line at ȳ, but does not rule out a curved relationship. A U-shaped pattern can have zero linear correlation.

{{math:transforms}}

If each x doubles, covariance doubles but x variance quadruples, so the slope halves. Adding 10 to every y shifts the line up 10. Centering does not swap the roles of x and y. An increase in x variance alone does not guarantee a smaller slope: the covariance may change too. Try these changes in the model lab below.

06 / 15 · THE RETAILER

Separate fitting from inference

{{regvisual:assumptions}}

Ready for the calculations? Open the original explanation below. Keep the assumptions with the result.

06Separate fitting from inferenceClassical assumptions and what BLUE really promises+

A fitted line is not yet an uncertainty model

Least squares gives coefficients for nearly any nondegenerate set of points. To attach standard errors, tests and intervals, we must describe how new samples could vary. That requires assumptions about the mean relationship and the errors. A computation can be correct while its uncertainty claims are wrong, for example when neighboring weeks share demand shocks.

{{math:assumptions}}

The classical presentation treats x values as fixed by design. For random predictors, state these conditions conditional on the observed X. The zero-conditional-mean condition rules out systematic omitted influences on y that track x. A straight-looking scatterplot does not prove it.

For Gauss–Markov

Under the displayed assumptions, OLS is best linear unbiased: a and b are unbiased and have the smallest variance among estimators that are both linear in the observed y values and unbiased. “Best” does not mean best among every possible estimator.

For exact small-sample t and F

Add independent normal errors with common variance, conditional on X. Equivalently use a joint normal error vector with covariance σ²I. Normality is not required to calculate OLS or for Gauss–Markov. Appropriate large-sample or robust methods can support inference under other conditions.

{{math:mean-y}}

The errors have zero mean. The responses generally do not. With normality and independence, each coefficient is also normal because it is a linear combination of the normal responses.

07 / 15 · THE RETAILER

Read the misses, estimate the noise

{{regvisual:residuals}}

Ready for the calculations? Open the original explanation below. Keep the assumptions with the result.

07Residuals are observed misses, not population errorsEstimate the noise, then inspect its pattern+

Use the misses to estimate what remains unknown

We cannot see the true population errors because we do not know the population line. We can inspect residuals around our fitted line as clues. Their size estimates unexplained response scatter; their pattern can warn that the mean or variance model is unsuitable. This step comes before interpreting a tiny slope p-value, not after the business decision.

{{math:error-residual}}

Two estimated coefficients use two degrees of freedom. Here SSE=5.785714, residual df=6, s²=.964286 and s=.981981 tens of orders, about 9.82 orders. s is a measure of unexplained response scatter, not the standard error of the slope.

Under the classical assumptions, E(s²|X)=σ². This does not imply E(s|X)=σ. The residuals satisfy ∑eᵢ=0 and ∑xᵢeᵢ=0 when an intercept is fitted, so they are constrained and generally correlated even when the underlying errors are independent.

Technical depth · Why n−2, and why leverage matters

{{math:residual-proof}}

A large predictor distance gives high leverage and can make an observation influential, especially with a large residual. High leverage alone is not proof that a point is wrong. Check the data and context rather than deleting points to improve significance.

Read the residual plot.

A curve suggests a misspecified mean. A funnel suggests changing variance. Runs over time suggest dependence. A single extreme point invites a data-quality and influence check. These are diagnostics, not proof that every assumption holds. Use an appropriate model, heteroskedasticity-robust or clustered uncertainty when justified; the lab’s intervals remain classical.

Pause and explain

The residual scatter s is about 9.82 orders. Is that the uncertainty of the slope?

Check your reasoning

No. It is response scatter around the fitted line, in orders. Slope uncertainty has units of orders per spending unit and also depends on the spread of x. Confusing the two would produce the wrong test and an interval with the wrong units.

08 / 15 · THE RETAILER

Give the slope an uncertainty range

{{regvisual:inference}}

Ready for the calculations? Open the original explanation below. Keep the assumptions with the result.

08How uncertain are the two coefficients?Standard errors, t tests and confidence intervals+

Why the estimated slope would change in another sample

A different set of market-weeks would yield a different line even if the population relationship stayed fixed. The slope SE measures that sampling variability, not the scatter of individual orders. Larger residual noise makes slopes less precise; a wider well-supported range of x helps distinguish a steep line from a shallow one. Now the Class 6 estimate-minus-null divided by SE logic returns.

{{math:coefficient-variance}}{{math:coefficient-se}}

Here sᵦ=.151523 and sₐ=.765153. More spread in the predictor, with the same noise and sample size, improves slope precision. Centering x makes the intercept the fitted mean at x̄, often a more useful and more precise baseline.

{{math:coefficient-tests}}

For H₀:β=0 against β≠0, t=12.727922 and p≈.00001443. At 5%, reject zero linear association under the model. The 95% slope interval is [1.557808, 2.299335] tens of orders per $100. It is not a causal-return interval.

For H₀:α=0, t=11.528973 and p≈.00002560. The 95% intercept interval is [6.949167,10.693691] tens of orders. Testing a baseline outside the observed x range may have little business relevance. Use a meaningful null value, not automatically zero. αsig denotes the significance level here, separate from the population intercept α.

Technical depth · Linear weights give the variances

{{math:linear-weights}}

Multiply each squared-weight sum by σ². The weight sums also give E(b|X)=β and E(a|X)=α. Under independent normal errors, the coefficient estimates are jointly normal and independent of SSE; (n−2)s²/σ² is chi-square with n−2 df. Dividing a centered normal estimate by its estimated standard error gives the exact t reference under its null.

Pause and explain

Does the slope interval prove that spending another $100 will cause about 15.6–23.0 more orders?

Check your reasoning

No. It quantifies uncertainty about a linear association under the sampling model. Confounding could explain part of that association. A causal spending decision needs an appropriate design, plus margins and advertising costs.

Split total variation

{{regvisual:partition}}

Ready for the calculations? Open the original explanation below. Keep the assumptions with the result.

09Explain variation with the fitted lineSST, SSR, SSE and the connection to ANOVA+

Reuse the prediction comparison from Class 7

ANOVA compared predicting with one grand mean against predicting with group means. Regression compares predicting with one grand mean against predicting with a line. In both cases, explained variation is the reduction in squared misses after adding structure. The remaining SSE is the same residual variation used to estimate noise above, not a separate kind of error.

{{math:decomposition}}

In our data, SST=162, SSR=156.214286, and SSE=5.785714, all in squared tens-of-orders units. Replacing the grand-mean prediction with the fitted line removes 156.214286 of squared prediction error.

Notation varies: SSR here means regression sum of squares; some software uses SSR for sum of squared residuals. Read the definition. An intercept is essential to this centered decomposition.

Technical depth · The cross term vanishes

{{math:decomp-proof}}

This equality is an algebraic fact about the fitted OLS line. It does not require normality or a causal interpretation.

10 / 15 · THE RETAILER

Read beyond the fit percentage

{{regvisual:fitshare}}

Ready for the calculations? Open the original explanation below. Keep the assumptions with the result.

10A good fit is not the whole storyR², correlation and what they cannot promise+

Ask how much improvement the line bought

A slope tells us change per unit of spend; R² answers a different question about fit within this sample. Divide the improvement over the grand-mean prediction by the total variation available to explain. The resulting share has no units, but loses the direction and business scale that the slope retained. Neither number substitutes for checking residuals or future performance.

{{math:r2}}

Here R²=.964286: the fitted line accounts for about 96.43% of the sample’s centered variation. It does not mean 96.43% of future orders will be predicted correctly, that the slope is causal, or that the model is adequate.

With an intercept and SST>0, in-sample R² lies between 0 and 1. A constant response has SST=0, so the ratio is undefined. Models without an intercept or out-of-sample R² do not share the same guarantee. R²=0 means no improvement over the sample mean by this straight line; a curved relationship can remain.

A high R² can coexist with extrapolation failure, biased sampling, correlated errors, or a bad causal interpretation. Judge residual patterns, practical effect size, uncertainty and performance on new data as well.

11 / 15 · THE RETAILER

Connect the two slope tests

{{regvisual:tests}}

Ready for the calculations? Open the original explanation below. Keep the assumptions with the result.

11Two tests, one answer about the slopeThe ANOVA F test equals the squared two-sided t test+

One predictor makes two familiar routes coincide

With one numeric predictor, the model’s only extra mean parameter beyond the intercept is the slope. Testing whether the line improves on a constant mean is therefore testing whether the slope is zero. The two-sided t route keeps the slope’s sign until the tail calculation; the F route squares it away. They should agree under the same model.

{{math:reg-f}}

Total df is n−1, regression df is 1, and error df is n−2. Our F is 162 with (1,6) df, and its upper-tail p≈.00001443 matches the two-sided slope t test. Squaring absorbs either positive or negative association into a large positive statistic.

{{math:f-t-proof}}

Technical depth · The reference distribution

{{math:f-distribution}}

The fitted mean is random across samples. Under β=0, E(ŷᵢ|X)=α; it is not an equality between an expectation and the realized sample ȳ. Expected mean squares need not equal their observed values, and a ratio of expectations is not generally the expectation of a ratio.

Estimate average demand

{{regvisual:mean}}

Ready for the calculations? Open the original explanation below. Keep the assumptions with the result.

12Estimate the average response at a chosen xA confidence interval for a population mean, not a new observation+

Choose the target before choosing the interval

For long-run staffing, the retailer might want average orders across many comparable weeks at a fixed spend. That target is the population mean response, so we need uncertainty about the estimated line at that x. We are not trying to capture each week’s fluctuation yet. The next topic adds that second uncertainty for a single future week.

{{math:mean-prediction}}

At x₀=4.5, advertising is $450 and the fitted mean is 17.5 tens of orders. The 95% confidence interval is [16.650475,18.349525], or about 166.5–183.5 orders for the population mean.

The interval is narrowest at x̄ and widens away from the data’s center. It describes the mean under this linear model, not the spread of all observations. Across repeated samples, 95% of intervals made by this method cover the fixed target mean when the assumptions hold.

Technical depth · Include coefficient covariance

{{math:mean-variance}}

These are pointwise intervals for a specified x₀. Plotting them at many x values does not create a simultaneous 95% confidence band over the whole line.

13 / 15 · THE RETAILER

Predict one new week

{{regvisual:individual}}

Ready for the calculations? Open the original explanation below. Keep the assumptions with the result.

13Predict one new outcomeA prediction interval must also allow for fresh noise+

One future week has a second source of uncertainty

Even if we knew the true mean line perfectly, an individual week could land above or below it. A prediction interval must allow both for estimating the line and for this new week’s own error. That is why using a mean confidence interval to plan capacity for one week would be overconfident.

{{math:individual-prediction}}

At the same $450 spend, a new market-week still has unpredictable order variation. The 95% prediction interval is [14.951425,20.048575] tens of orders, or about 149.5–200.5 individual weekly orders. It is wider than the mean confidence interval because it includes both estimation uncertainty and fresh noise.

The future error must be independent of the fitting data, with the same variance and normal model for this exact interval. A repeat measurement correlated with an existing observation needs a different formula. The interval predicts a new response; it is not a confidence interval for a parameter.

Technical depth · Where the extra 1 comes from

{{math:prediction-proof}}

More data can make mean estimation very precise, but the new outcome’s own noise does not disappear. Neither interval accounts for an unmodeled change in the business process.

Pause and explain

Which interval would you report for one new $450 market-week?

Check your reasoning

The prediction interval, about 149.5–200.5 orders under the model, because one week has its own unpredictable error. The narrower 166.5–183.5 interval estimates average demand. Neither guarantees capacity protection or covers an unmodeled business change.

Review, transfer task and knowledge check

Return to advertising and demand

In the synthetic sample, the fitted association is about 19.29 additional orders per $100. The slope interval and tests show precision under the classical model, not a guaranteed causal return. For $450 spend, estimate average demand near 175 orders; use the wider prediction interval, about 149.5–200.5 orders, when discussing one new comparable market-week. Capacity decisions also need service-risk costs, and a 95% prediction interval is not a guarantee.

A justified recommendation is to use the line as a conditional planning baseline within the observed spend range, inspect residuals and future performance, and seek causal evidence before attributing extra orders to extra spending. Do not recommend a higher advertising budget from the slope alone.

Transfer task: forecast a store’s staffing need

A store fits sales on foot traffic. The slope is significant and R² is high. Name three checks before predicting tomorrow, and choose an interval for tomorrow’s sales rather than average sales over many similar days.

Compare your reasoning

Check whether tomorrow’s traffic is within the observed range, whether the mean/residual patterns and sampling dependence support the model, and whether seasonality or a changed business process undermines transfer. Use a prediction interval for tomorrow’s individual sales, not a confidence interval for the conditional mean. High R² does not replace these checks or prove that increasing traffic causes the fitted sales gain.

Written practice and solutions
  1. Double every x. The slope halves, intercept and fitted values stay the same, as do residuals, R² and the zero-slope t/F tests.
  2. Shift every y upward by 10. The intercept rises 10. The slope and its zero-null test are unchanged; the intercept’s zero-null test changes.
  3. Predict at the sample mean x̄. The fitted mean is ȳ and its standard error is s/√n. A new individual uses s√(1+1/n).
  4. R²=0 for a curved pattern. There may still be strong nonlinear association. Inspect the scatter and residual plots.
  5. A significant slope. This rejects the specified zero-slope null under the model; it does not prove causation or business importance.
  6. A future point outside the data range. Report extrapolation, check whether the model is credible there, and do not treat the model interval as protection from structural change.
