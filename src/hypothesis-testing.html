01Start with a decision, not a p-valueNull, alternative, simple and composite hypotheses+

Why estimate first, then test?

A manager has two different needs: estimate how long dispatch takes, and decide whether the evidence crosses a pre-agreed warning threshold. An interval answers “how much, with what uncertainty?” A test answers a narrower question about a benchmark. We need both before recommending action. The 24-hour promise below introduces the question; later minute-based cases practice the same reasoning in separate processes.

A fulfillment manager suspects that average dispatch time now exceeds a promised 24 hours. An auditor, by contrast, wants to know whether a reported average is inaccurate in either direction. Both questions compare a population parameter with a benchmark, but they require different tests.

{{math:hypotheses}}

Null hypothesis H₀

The working model used to calculate how surprising a statistic would be. It usually includes equality to a benchmark. It is not a fact established in advance.

Alternative H₁ or Hₐ

The competing claim about the same population parameter. It is mutually exclusive with the null and defines which departures count as evidence.

Simple or composite?

A parameter claim fixing one value is simple in this lesson’s terminology; an interval, inequality or “not equal” claim is composite.

Choose the direction before seeing the data. Use a right-sided alternative for an increase, a left-sided alternative for a decrease, and a two-sided alternative when either departure matters. Selecting the more favorable tail after observing the result breaks the intended error control.

For the dispatch decision, H₀: μ=24 versus H₁: μ>24 asks about deterioration. For an accuracy audit, H₀: μ=24 versus H₁: μ≠24 catches both overstatement and understatement. “The sample mean differs” is not a population hypothesis: sample means almost always differ a little.

Technical depth · what “simple” leaves out

A fully simple statistical hypothesis fixes the entire distribution. A claim about μ alone can still leave a nuisance parameter, such as σ², unspecified. The course’s “simple null” terminology refers to the tested parameter. A directional test is often formally written H₀: μ≤24 versus μ>24; its rejection probability is calibrated at the least-favorable boundary μ=24. This lesson writes that boundary equality explicitly.

02 / 12 · THE DECISION

Weigh the two errors

{{hypvisual:power}}

Five percent of which studies?

Use the chart’s 64-observation plan, not the separate 24-hour dispatch case. Imagine a manager repeating the same study with fresh independent samples. Each study measures 64 processing times and produces one decision. Assume IID normal times with known population SD 8 minutes, a 50-minute null benchmark and a predeclared right-sided test at α = .05.

First translate the rule into minutes: SE = 8 ÷ √64 = 1 minute. The right-tail critical value is about 1.6449, so the test rejects when the sample mean exceeds about 51.6449 minutes. Keep that same rule in both imagined worlds below; the true process mean is what changes.

  1. No real delay: true mean 50 minutes. Among 1,000 repetitions in this world, expect about 50 false alarms and 950 non-rejections. The denominator for α is all studies where the null is true, not only studies that raise an alarm.
  2. A real two-minute delay: true mean 52 minutes. The same cutoff is (51.6449 − 52) ÷ 1 ≈ −0.3551 SE from this mean. About 63.9% of sample means exceed it. Among 1,000 repetitions in this world, expect about 639 detections and 361 misses. Power counts detections; β counts misses. Their denominator is all studies at this specified true alternative.

These are expected, rounded counts, not guaranteed totals. The 1,000 counts studies; 64 counts observations within each study. The two worlds are separate thought experiments, not a claim that half of real processes have a delay.

Predict: if a manager sees 100 alarms from real studies using α = .05, must 5 of those alarms be false?

Check the denominator before interpreting an alarm

No. Five percent describes alarms among true-null studies, not true nulls among alarms. To assess the false-alarm share of all alarms, we would also need information about how common the true states are and how often the test detects the relevant alternatives. Neither α nor power alone tells us the probability that one reported finding is true.

02Two ways to make the wrong callSignificance, Type I / II errors and power at an alternative+

A decision rule has consequences

Once the direction is fixed, ask what a wrong decision would cost. For the 24-hour promise, a false alarm means escalating a process that meets the benchmark; a missed signal means leaving real deterioration unaddressed. Choose an acceptable false-alarm rate before observing the sample, then ask whether the design can detect a delay that actually matters.

Imagine pausing a dispatch process when a test detects deterioration. A false alarm wastes capacity; missing real deterioration costs customers. A test cannot promise to avoid both errors.

{{math:errors}}

In the convention where “positive” means rejecting H₀, Type I is a false positive and Type II is a false negative. The significance level α is a chosen long-run false-rejection allowance, commonly .10, .05 or .01, not a probability that this particular decision is wrong. For discrete or approximate tests, the actual Type I rate can differ from the nominal α.

For a fixed design and effect, reducing α generally makes rejection harder and reduces power. Power is not one universal property of a test: it depends on the specific alternative effect, sample size, variability, design and test. More independent data, better measurement, effective pairing or a better-suited design can improve power without raising α. Increasing sample size is not the only route.

Predict → change → observe → explain: Detect a meaningful delay

Predict whether doubling n will improve detection of the chosen two-minute delay without changing α. Click “Double sample size” and compare power and the rejection threshold. Explain: the mean becomes less noisy, so a fixed real delay is easier to detect; the false-alarm allowance stays the same. Reset n to 64, then reduce α to .01: why does power fall? You can also set the true mean on the null side of a one-sided test: then the displayed rejection probability is a false-alarm rate, not detection of an alternative, and non-rejection is correct rather than a Type II error.

For a right-sided known-σ mean test, reject when X̄ exceeds c. Under a particular alternative μ₁, standardize c using μ₁ rather than μ₀. The resulting upper-tail probability is power:

{{math:power}}

At μ₁=μ₀ this equals α. As the positive effect grows, it increases toward one. The confidence coefficient 1−α is not the same as power 1−β. A non-rejection with low power can be entirely compatible with a consequential effect.

Keep the tail with the cutoff

Class 5 labels its positive critical value by upper-tail area: z.025 ≈ +1.96. This class labels quantiles by lower-tail probability: the same positive cutoff is z.975 ≈ +1.96, while z.025 ≈ −1.96. The normal curve has not changed; the label has.

The same translation applies to t: Class 5’s positive tν,.025 is this class’s tν,.975. Say “the cutoff with 2.5% above it” or “97.5% below it” before reading a table or using an inverse CDF. For a two-sided 5% test, use both a negative left cutoff and a positive right cutoff.

03Turn “surprising” into a reproducible ruleTest statistics, critical values, tails and p-values+

From a raw difference to comparable evidence

A two-minute delay is not equally persuasive in every sample. If sample means normally fluctuate by one minute, it is two standard errors away; if they fluctuate by four minutes, it is only half a standard error away. Standardization measures the observed gap in units of sampling noise. The null reference curve then tells us how unusual that gap would be if the benchmark model held.

  1. State the question. Specify the population, parameter, null, alternative, α and analysis before collecting or examining outcomes.
  2. Check the design and model. Identify independent or paired observations, the sampling unit, normality or large-sample justification, and what variability is known.
  3. Standardize the evidence. Calculate a test statistic: typically (estimate−null value)/standard error, or a variance ratio.
  4. Compare and explain. Use either a critical region or a p-value, then report the estimate, uncertainty, assumptions and practical consequences.

A rejection region is the set of statistic values that trigger rejection. Its complement is the non-rejection region, not proof that the null is true. Critical values mark the boundary and are calculated under H₀ using the chosen α.

A p-value is the probability, under the null model, of a statistic at least as extreme as the observed statistic in the directions defined by H₁. In the continuous tests here, it is also the smallest significance level at which the observed result would reject. Before sampling it is random; afterward it is a calculated number.

Reject when p≤α; otherwise do not reject. A p-value is not P(H₀ is true), the chance the result occurred “by chance,” the probability of replication, or the size of the effect.

04A mean test when population SD is knownOne-sample z, three alternatives and the normal model+

Start with the simplest reference model

We first make an unusually strong assumption: the population spread is already known. This lets us isolate the logic of testing without also estimating noise. Read the next calculation as three steps: find the gap from 30, find the uncertainty of the sample mean, then divide. Do not compare the gap directly with the spread of individual orders.

Suppose independent order-processing times follow a normal population with a genuinely known SD of 6 minutes. A random sample of 36 orders has mean 32 minutes; the benchmark is 30. The standard error is 1 minute, so z=2.

{{math:z}}

At α=.05, a predeclared right-sided test has cutoff 1.6449 and p≈.0228: reject the 30-minute null in favor of an increase. A two-sided question has cutoffs ±1.9600 and p≈.0455: reject equality. A left-sided question has p≈.9772: this positive statistic does not support a decrease. These are different research questions, not a menu to choose from after seeing z.

For a sample mean of 28 instead, z=−2. A predeclared left-sided test rejects below −1.6449 with p≈.0228. The two-sided p-value remains .0455. The raw-mean rejection thresholds are 30±1.6449 for the relevant one-sided direction, or 30±1.9600 for the two-sided test.

The normal distribution of X̄ is exact for IID normal observations. With a nonnormal population, the CLT can justify an approximation when sample size and tail behavior support it. A historical SD estimate is not automatically a known population SD. Dependence, strong skewness and outliers can invalidate a casual normal approximation.

05Estimate the SD, change the reference curveOne-sample t, exact normal inference and the Wald limit+

Now remove the unrealistic certainty

The previous example knew the population SD. In a normal business dataset, our estimate of the mean and our estimate of its noise both change from sample to sample. The t curve allows for that second source of uncertainty. The calculation still measures gap divided by SE; what changes is the reference distribution and its degrees of freedom.

Most operational datasets do not come with a known population variance. Estimate it from the sample, and account for that extra uncertainty with Student t.

{{math:t}}

For a normal population, a random sample of 16 service jobs has mean 52 minutes and sample SD 4. Testing the 50-minute benchmark gives SE=1 and t=2 with 15 degrees of freedom. A right-sided test uses t15,.95≈1.7531; a two-sided test uses ±t15,.975≈±2.1314. The same statistic can reject a predeclared increase but not reject a two-sided difference at 5%.

For a left-sided test, the cutoff is negative: −tν,1−α. All p-values follow the table in Topic 3 with the relevant t CDF. The heavier tails make t critical values larger in magnitude than z’s at small degrees of freedom.

The lab starts with a known-SD example. Choose “Try the small-sample t example” to reproduce the 16-job case above, then compare directions and methods.

Predict → change → observe → explain: Same data, different question

Choose “Try the small-sample t example,” then select the right-sided alternative. Predict whether switching to a two-sided alternative can alter the decision while leaving t unchanged. Observe both p-values and cutoffs. Explain: two tails share the false-alarm allowance, so evidence in one direction must be stronger. Technical depth · exact t and asymptotic z are different arguments

{{math:t-proof}}

Normal sampling makes the sample mean and variance independent. That independence, together with the chi-square law of the variance, proves the exact t distribution. Normality of the mean alone is not the entire proof.

{{math:wald}}

Without population normality, IID observations with positive finite variance can support a large-sample Wald test using s and normal critical values. This is an asymptotic statement, not an exact finite-sample normal law. There is no universal sample-size threshold that cures severe skewness, extreme tails or dependence. A t procedure is often used as an approximation too, but its exact guarantee is the normal-sampling result.

06A test and an interval can tell the same storyTwo-sided inversion, one-sided bounds and matching methods+

Recover the size of the problem

A reject/do-not-reject label compresses information. Return to Class 5 and ask which other benchmark values would survive the same two-sided test. Collecting those values gives the matching confidence interval. This is why reporting an interval alongside a test helps a manager judge both evidence and possible magnitude.

Class 5 asked which parameter values are compatible with the sample. A test asks whether one particular value is compatible. When both calculations use the same model, standard error and critical value, they are two views of the same calculation.

{{math:ci}}

For the 32-minute known-SD example, the 95% interval is [30.0400, 33.9600]. It excludes 30, matching rejection by the two-sided 5% z test. For the 16-job t example, the 95% interval is approximately [49.8686, 54.1314]; it contains 50, matching non-rejection.

{{math:onebound}}

A right-sided α test corresponds to a 100(1−α)% lower one-sided confidence bound: reject when that bound exceeds the null value. A left-sided test uses an upper bound. A one-sided 5% test is not paired with a two-sided 95% interval.

At exact equality with a critical value, this page uses p≤α for rejection. Open versus closed confidence endpoints only change that zero-probability boundary in continuous models. In numerical work, do not make decisions from rounded printed values.

Matching matters. A pooled two-proportion score test and an unpooled Wald interval use different SEs and need not agree exactly. Equivalence tests are also different: failing to reject a zero effect does not establish that an effect is small enough to be operationally equivalent.

Pause and explain

The 16-job 95% interval includes 50. Has the process met its benchmark?

Check your reasoning

No. The two-sided test does not reject 50, but values above 50 also remain compatible with these data. Non-rejection is not certification; compare the interval with a practically acceptable delay and gather better evidence if the decision needs more precision.

07Test a percentage against a promiseBinary trials, null variance and approximation checks+

Change the outcome, keep the logic

Dispatch time is numeric; meeting a service promise is yes/no. We still compare an estimate with a benchmark in SE units, but the sampling variability now comes from a binary outcome. Imagine the promise really holds: that null model supplies the expected success and failure counts and the SE for this test.

A support team promises that 90% of eligible tickets receive a first reply within a day. Code each independently sampled ticket as 1 for meeting the target and 0 otherwise. The population probability π is the parameter; the observed fraction p̂ is its estimate.

{{math:proportion}}

The denominator uses π₀ from the null, not the observed fraction. Under a 90% promise with 200 tickets, the expected null counts are 180 successes and 20 failures. A cautious classroom screen asks for at least 10 of each, though no count rule guarantees accuracy.

To investigate underperformance, specify H₁: π<.90 in advance and use the left normal tail. For any discrepancy use two tails; for improvement use the right tail. The same critical-value and p-value rules apply as for a z mean test.

Predict → change → observe → explain: Keep the benchmark separate from the estimate

This lab uses a separate conversion case: 66 of 200 visitors against a 25% benchmark. Predict the direction of evidence before reading the p-value. Change conversions to 50 while keeping n=200 and π₀=.25. Observe z=0. Explain why a sample exactly at the benchmark supplies no evidence of improvement, even though it does not prove the benchmark true.

{{proportion-lab}}

Technical depth · the Bernoulli route

{{math:prop-proof}}

Independent, identical Bernoulli trials give the binomial count model. Standardizing its mean and variance under π=π₀ gives the normal approximation. For sparse expected counts or probabilities near 0 or 1, use a suitable exact binomial or calibrated alternative. The formula needs 0<π₀<1; a degenerate boundary null needs separate treatment.

The basic Wald confidence interval from Class 5 substitutes p̂ into its SE, while this null-based score test substitutes π₀. Inverting the score test gives the Wilson interval, not the simple Wald interval. Binary outcomes clustered within customers need a design that accounts for clustering.

08First decide what “two samples” meansIndependent groups, matched pairs and the difference parameter+

Choose from the study design before any formula

The manager now asks whether a proposed change performs better than the current process. First identify the unit: an order, visitor or employee. Then ask whether the same unit appears in both measurements. Two spreadsheet columns do not answer that question. Keeping or discarding pairing changes the uncertainty calculation, so a convenient software menu must not choose the design for you.

A retailer compares basket values across two independently randomized checkout designs. A warehouse compares each employee’s completion time before and after new training. Both involve two columns, but only the first is an independent-samples problem. In the second, the row matching contains information.

Independent means

Different independent sampling units in the two groups. Use Welch, or pooled t when a common variance is justified.

Paired means

The same unit twice, or deliberately matched units. Analyze each pair’s difference, not two unrelated columns.

Binary or variability?

Conversion rates need proportion methods. A question about dispersion, rather than location, needs a variance method.

{{math:groups}}{{math:difference}}

Let Δ=μ₁−μ₂ and test H₀: Δ=Δ₀. Equality is Δ₀=0, but a business benchmark can be nonzero. A positive statistic supports Δ>Δ₀; a negative one supports Δ<Δ₀. Label the subtraction order in every report.

Technical depth · why a difference adds variances

For any A and B, Var(A−B)=Var(A)+Var(B)−2Cov(A,B). Independent groups make the covariance zero. Each sample mean has variance σⱼ²/nⱼ, giving the expression above. Normal populations make the difference exactly normal; appropriate CLTs can provide a large-sample approximation. Independence is a design assumption, not something a p-value can certify.

Pause and explain

The same 20 employees use each system. Which independent-sample test should you choose?

Check your reasoning

Neither by default. Preserve each employee’s paired observations and analyze the 20 differences. A paired design concerns within-employee change. If there are additional repeated trials or carryover effects, the basic paired model also needs reconsideration.

09Pool information only when the model allows itEqual population variances, pooled t and its proof+

What can legitimately be combined?

For independent groups, the mean difference is our signal. We still need its noise. If both populations genuinely share a variance, each group teaches us about the same noise parameter, so pooling improves its estimate. We pool within-group deviations, not the groups’ means or their raw observations around one common center.

If independent normal groups share one population variance, both sample variances estimate the same quantity. Combine their within-group evidence, weighting by degrees of freedom.

{{math:pooled}}

This is not the variance of all observations around one grand mean. Each group is centered at its own mean, so between-group differences do not inflate the estimate of common within-group variance. The larger group receives greater weight because it contributes more residual degrees of freedom.

{{math:pooled-test}}{{math:pooled-ci}}

Choose the relevant right, left or two-sided t rule from Topic 3 with ν=n₁+n₂−2. Report the estimated difference in its original units and the matching interval. Equal population variances do not require exactly equal sample variances.

Build the noise estimate before dividing the gap

Use the original lesson’s numerical example in a synthetic service setting, separate from the lab defaults: Team A has 50 independent jobs, mean 40 minutes and sample SD 4; Team B has 40 independent jobs, mean 42 minutes and sample SD 3. For this pooled calculation, assume independent normal populations with a common variance. Test A minus B against zero, two-sided at 5%.

  1. Recover the within-team sums of squares. A’s variance is 16 minutes² and its residual degrees of freedom are 49, giving 784 minutes². B contributes 39 × 9 = 351 minutes². Each team has used one degree of freedom to estimate its own mean.
  2. Combine information about the same variance. The two sums add to 1135 minutes²; divide by 49 + 39 = 88. The weights are 49/88 and 39/88, not one half each.{{math:depth-pooled-variance}}
  3. Move from individual spread to uncertainty in a difference. The pooled SD is about 3.5913 minutes. It is not the SE of the gap: each mean averages several jobs, so multiply the pooled variance by 1/50 + 1/40 before taking a square root.{{math:depth-pooled-test}}
  4. Return to the business unit. With 88 df, the two-sided p-value is about .0102 and the 95% interval for A minus B is [−3.5140, −0.4860] minutes. The model supports a lower population mean for A, with a two-minute sample gap. Whether that saving is worth changing teams’ processes also depends on costs and study design.
Check: why not average the two SDs or use 90 degrees of freedom?

Variances, not SDs, combine through sums of squared residuals. Averaging 4 and 3 bypasses that calculation. The 90 jobs contribute 88 residual degrees of freedom because two separate group means were estimated. A pooled variance describes within-team noise; centering all 90 jobs on a single grand mean would also count the difference between the teams.

Technical depth · the full pooled argument

{{math:pooled-proof}}

Each group contributes (nⱼ−1)Sⱼ²/σ², a chi-square variable with nⱼ−1 degrees of freedom. Independence across groups makes their sum chi-square with ν=n₁+n₂−2. Normal sampling makes each group’s mean independent of its variance; group independence then makes the mean difference independent of the pooled residual sum.

The numerator is divided by σ times √(1/n₁+1/n₂), its true SD. Dividing that standard normal variable by √(U/ν) replaces σ with Sₚ and yields t. Under the common-variance model E(Sₚ²)=σ²; without that model, the exact derivation fails.

10Let the two groups have different variabilityWelch’s t, fractional degrees of freedom and approximation+

What if the shared-variance story is doubtful?

A low-volume store and a high-volume store may have different customer mixes and spreads. Rather than force one noise estimate on both, Welch combines their separate contributions to the mean difference’s uncertainty. The choice rests on design and a defensible model, not on searching for whichever test produces significance.

Store A and Store B may have very different spreads in order value. Welch’s test estimates each group’s contribution to uncertainty separately. It is a useful default for independent mean comparisons when equal population variances are not substantively justified.

{{math:welch}}{{math:welch-ci}}

Use the t tail rules with this ν. Keep fractional degrees of freedom in software; do not routinely floor them. A printed table may force a conservative lower-df approximation, but that is a table convenience, not the definition of the method.

Keep the same gap; change the variance model

Reuse the original 50-job/40-job example immediately above. Keep A’s mean 40, SD 4 and B’s mean 42, SD 3, but do not impose equal population variances. Independence and a defensible sampling model still matter.

  1. Ask what each mean contributes. A contributes 16/50 = .320 minutes² to the estimated variance of the gap. B contributes 9/40 = .225 minutes². Independent means add these contributions, even though the means themselves are subtracted.{{math:depth-welch-se}}
  2. Account for uncertainty in those contributions. A’s variance estimate has 49 residual df; B’s has 39. Welch’s formula combines both estimates and their uncertainty into an effective reference df. It is not a count of people and need not be an integer.{{math:depth-welch-df}}
  3. Use that reference, then interpret. The statistic is −2/.7382 ≈ −2.7091 using unrounded quantities. With about 87.6730 df, the two-sided p-value is .0081 and the 95% interval is [−3.4672, −0.5328] minutes. The observed −2-minute gap has not changed; its estimated noise and reference distribution have.

Here Welch’s SE is slightly smaller than the pooled SE of .7618, but that ordering is not guaranteed. The two methods encode different variance assumptions. Do not adopt an assumption merely because its p-value is smaller.

Check: do the 50 observations guarantee that A’s mean is more precise?

No. A has more observations but also a larger sample SD. Its contribution .320 exceeds B’s .225. Precision depends on spread relative to sample size, not on sample size alone. {{independent-lab}}

Welch is not a fix for dependent observations, poor sampling, highly influential outliers or arbitrary small nonnormal samples. Its t reference is approximate even for unequal-variance normal populations. Avoid mechanically choosing pooled versus Welch by first running a significance test for equal variances; that two-stage selection has its own error behavior.

Technical depth · what Welch actually approximates

{{math:welch-proof}}{{math:welch-match}}

For a normal sample, Var(Sⱼ²)=2σⱼ⁴/(nⱼ−1). Independence makes the variances of S₁²/n₁ and S₂²/n₂ add. Matching the first two moments of V to a scaled chi-square gives an effective ν. Substitute sample variances for unknown population variances to obtain the practical formula.

The normal standardized difference is independent of the group variances under normal sampling. Combining it with the approximately scaled-chi-square denominator motivates an approximate t ratio. The variance sum V is not t distributed; it is nonnegative. It is the standardized mean difference TW that receives the approximate t reference.

The eight differences are the sample

Keep the original eight employees matched by ID. Their before-minus-after differences are 3, 4, 0, 5, 4, 1, 3, 4 minutes. A positive value means time saved. Their sum is 24, so the mean saving is 24/8 = 3 minutes.

  1. Center the differences: subtract 3 from each, giving 0, 1, −3, 2, 1, −2, 0, 1 minutes.
  2. Square and add: 0 + 1 + 9 + 4 + 1 + 4 + 0 + 1 = 20 minutes².
  3. Estimate the spread: sD² = 20/(8 − 1) = 20/7, so sD ≈ 1.6903 minutes.
  4. Estimate the uncertainty: SE = √(20/7)/√8 ≈ 0.5976 minutes. Against a zero mean saving, t = (3 − 0)/0.5976 ≈ 5.020, with 7 degrees of freedom. Use unrounded values in the calculation.

Predict: why not use n = 16 because there are eight before and eight after measurements?

Check the unit and keep the pairs together

The analysis has eight differences, one per employee, not 16 independent people. Calculate the SD from those differences, not from either original column. The exact paired t model assumes independent differences from the same normal distribution across employees; the two measurements within an employee are intentionally paired.

If a before or after value is missing, do not close up one column and subtract the next row. Match by ID and identify complete pairs. For this ordinary complete-pair analysis, n counts those complete pairs. Report exclusions and assess whether missingness could bias the result; keeping only complete pairs does not automatically make them representative.

11Keep each before with its own afterDifferences, covariance, exact assumptions and paired t+

Turn matching into the right outcome

Imagine one employee is consistently slow and another consistently fast. Comparing each person with themselves removes much of that between-person variation. Create one “before minus after” value per employee, then test the mean of those differences. This is a one-sample problem after the outcome has been correctly constructed, not an independent two-sample problem with twice as much data.

Suppose 8 warehouse employees each complete a comparable task before and after training. Define D=before−after, so a positive difference means a reduction in time. Preserve the rows: shuffling the “after” column would destroy the pairing.

{{math:paired}}{{math:paired-ci}}

Check the differences, not just the two columns. Different pairs must be independent. The exact small-sample paired t model requires normally distributed differences; the before and after measurements do not each need a separate normality claim. With small samples, examine unusual differences carefully. Pairing alone does not show that training caused the change.

The sample size n counts pairs, not 2n measurements. Test H₀: μD=Δ₀ using the one-sample t rules. For a predeclared improvement in this sign convention, use μD>0. Reversing the subtraction reverses the directional alternative and statistic; a two-sided conclusion is unchanged.

Predict → change → observe → explain: The row matching is part of the data

First calculate the first employee’s difference: 32−29=3 minutes. Predict what happens if you add 10 to every before value and the matching after value. Edit both lists accordingly. Observe that all differences, their SD and the test stay unchanged. Explain: paired inference concerns within-person changes, not the absolute level of the two columns. Counting 16 measurements as 16 pairs would invent information.

{{paired-lab}}

Technical depth · the dependence is useful, not optional

Different pairs must be independent for the ordinary paired t test. Within a pair, dependence is expected and is included through the covariance term. Positive covariance can reduce the variance of the difference substantially; using the independent-groups SE would discard that information.

An exact finite-sample t test requires the differences Dᵢ to be IID normal. Joint normality of each pair is a sufficient condition for normal differences, but normal marginal distributions alone are not enough. Once D is normal, its sample mean and sample variance have the same independent normal/chi-square construction as Topic 5. A sufficiently well-behaved large sample can justify an approximation without exact normal differences.

Before/after change alone does not establish that training caused the change: time trends, practice, changed tasks and selection can confound a causal claim. Pairing handles a statistical dependence structure; it does not create randomization.

12Compare conversion rates without mixing up the SEsPooled equality test, unpooled interval and binary assumptions+

Move from amounts to conversion decisions

A checkout experiment may change basket value or the chance of any purchase. These are different outcomes and require different methods. For conversion, define one eligible visitor as one independent trial. Under equal population conversion rates, both groups estimate one probability, which explains why the equality test pools counts even though the interval below does not.

Two checkout designs are randomly assigned to independent visitors. Each visitor contributes one conversion outcome. To test whether the population conversion probabilities are equal, pool successes and trials under that equality model.

{{math:twoprop}}

The null is π₁=π₂. It does not say that the observed proportions p̂₁ and p̂₂ must be equal. Unequal sample sizes are allowed; the pooled fraction weights by sample sizes rather than averaging the two percentages equally.

Check the null expected successes and failures nⱼp̂pool and nⱼ(1−p̂pool) in each group. A common cautious screen is at least 10 each. Sparse data call for a more suitable method. Repeated visits by the same person, overlapping groups and matched binary outcomes do not meet the independent-binomial model.

Predict → change → observe → explain: Separate percentage points from evidence

At the default 84/400 versus 60/400, calculate the gap before viewing the result: .21−.15=.06, or 6 percentage points. Predict what changes if you double both counts and both sample sizes. Observe that the gap stays fixed but the SE shrinks and evidence strengthens. Explain why the effect did not become more valuable just because its p-value became smaller.

{{twoprop-lab}}{{math:twoprop-ci}}

The basic confidence interval above does not assume equal population proportions, so its SE is unpooled. It is a large-sample Wald interval and can behave poorly near boundaries. Express a difference of .04 as 4 percentage points; it is not necessarily a 4% relative improvement.

Two percentages, two standard errors

Use the original lesson’s unequal-size example as a separate synthetic checkout study: A converts 30 of 50 independent visitors; B converts 28 of 40. Predeclare a two-sided 5% test of equal population conversion probabilities.

  1. Keep the effect separate from the null estimate. The observed rates are .60 and .70, so A minus B is −.10, or −10 percentage points. Under equality, estimate one common probability from 58 conversions among all 90 visitors.{{math:depth-prop-pool}}

    The simple average of .60 and .70 is .65. That gives the smaller group the same weight as the larger one. Pooling counts gives each visitor the same weight.

  2. Check the null model in both groups. Expected successes/failures are about 32.22/17.78 for A and 25.78/14.22 for B. All exceed the classroom screen of 10, supporting use of the normal approximation alongside the independent-trial assumptions. These are expected counts, so they need not be integers.
  3. Use the common probability to test equality. The null SE is about .10154, so z ≈ −.9848 and the two-sided p-value is about .3247: do not reject. A ten-percentage-point observed gap is only about one null SE from zero.{{math:depth-prop-test}}
  4. Release equality when estimating the gap. For the basic Wald interval, allow each population to have its own probability and estimate its variance separately.{{math:depth-prop-interval}}

    The 95% interval is about [−29.65, 9.65] percentage points. It permits a substantial disadvantage for A and some advantage for A. Non-rejection does not establish that the checkout designs perform equally well.

Check: can one SE serve both calculations here?

No. The equality test asks how unusual the gap is in a common-rate model; the interval estimates a gap that may be nonzero. The null SE is .10154 and the unpooled interval SE is .10025 in this example. Both calculations include zero as plausible here, but their different constructions need not agree at every significance boundary. Use unrounded values and label which calculation the SE belongs to.

Technical depth · constrain the population, not the data

{{math:twoprop-var}}

Under equality, the combined binomial likelihood is proportional to πk₁+k₂(1−π)n₁+n₂−k₁−k₂. Differentiating its log and setting the derivative to zero gives the pooled estimate. This justifies pooling under H₀ without asserting that the two observed proportions are equal.

The pooled and unpooled estimated SEs are based on different models. The pooled estimate is not guaranteed to be smaller in every dataset. The pooled equality test and unpooled Wald interval are not exact inverses. A nonzero hypothesized difference needs a method constructed for that null; simply inserting a nonzero numerator into this equality-pooled formula is not generally justified.

13Is one process more variable?F tests, asymmetric tails, reciprocal checks and proof+

Sometimes consistency is the business question

Equal average fill can hide a costly problem: one line may produce many more underfilled and overfilled packages. Mean tests cannot answer that spread question. A ratio compares the two population variances, with equality represented by one rather than zero. Keep this F test distinct from next class’s F test about several means.

Two packaging lines may have the same average fill but different consistency. For independent samples from normal populations, compare their variances using F. Keep the numerator group fixed so that “greater variability” has an unambiguous meaning.

{{math:f-test}}{{math:f-tails}}

GF is the F CDF and QF its lower-tail quantile function with the stated numerator and denominator df. F is nonnegative and right-skewed. A two-sided test has α/2 in each tail, but its critical values are not symmetric around one.

Predict → change → observe → explain: Reverse the comparison without changing the question

With SDs 6 and 4, predict the variance ratio: 36/16=2.25, not 6/4. Keep a two-sided alternative and click “Swap A and B.” Observe the reciprocal ratio, swapped df and unchanged two-sided p-value. Explain why the evidence about unequal variability stays the same although the named direction reverses.

{{variance-lab}}

Follow the smaller tail when the ratio flips

Use the original lesson’s variance example in a separate synthetic packaging setting: A has 50 independent measurements and sample variance 30 grams²; B has 40 and sample variance 20 grams². Assume independent normal populations and test equal population variances, two-sided at 5%.

  1. Fix the group order. A’s variance divided by B’s is 30/20 = 1.5. The reference distribution has 49 numerator df and 39 denominator df. A ratio of 1 is the null benchmark; the statistic is never negative.
  2. Measure both tail areas. The lower tail at 1.5 is about .90363 and the upper tail is .09637. The equal-tail two-sided p-value is twice the smaller area: about .19274. It is not .09637 just because the observed ratio exceeds 1.
  3. Check the critical-region route. The lower-tail .025 and .975 quantiles are about .55303 and 1.84629. The observed 1.5 lies between them, agreeing with non-rejection. The cutoffs are not 1 plus and minus the same distance: F is not symmetric.
  4. Reverse both the statistic and its reference. For B divided by A, the statistic becomes 2/3 and the df become 39, 49. Now the lower tail is .09637.{{math:depth-f-reciprocal}}

    The new critical limits are about .54163 and 1.80821, obtained by reciprocating the old upper and lower limits in reverse order. The two-sided p-value stays .19274: relabeling the same lines cannot create new evidence.

Check: does non-rejection certify equal consistency?

No. The sample variance ratio is 1.5, but these data do not reject equality at 5% under the model. That is not proof of equal population spreads. Also distinguish variance from SD: a variance ratio of 1.5 corresponds to an SD ratio of √1.5 ≈ 1.2247, not an SD that is 50% larger. Neither statement compares mean fill amounts.

Technical depth · construction and reciprocal invariance

{{math:f-proof}}{{math:f-reciprocal}}

Swapping groups replaces f by 1/f and swaps the degrees of freedom. The two-sided p-value is unchanged, while right and left alternatives exchange roles. Critical limits reciprocate in reverse order; do not take a reciprocal while leaving df unchanged.

{{math:f-ci}}

The variance-ratio interval is obtained by solving the F pivot inequality for σ₁²/σ₂². This extends the same test-inversion idea used for means.

Normality matters especially here. The classical F variance test is sensitive to nonnormality and outliers. Consider a robust spread comparison such as Levene or Brown–Forsythe when appropriate, with design and interpretation checked separately. Non-rejection does not prove equal variances, and a rejected variance test does not answer a question about means.

Write the recommendation

{{hypvisual:decision}}

Not significant: too small to matter, or too uncertain to tell?

Consider a separate workflow study. Define the population improvement as old time minus new time, so positive values mean faster service. Before collecting data, the manager says a saving of at least 2 minutes would justify rollout. Compare two hypothetical, properly constructed 95% two-sided confidence intervals for that same improvement:

  • Study A: −0.5 to 0.5 minutes.
  • Study B: −3 to 5 minutes.

Predict: both contain zero. Must they lead to the same recommendation about a two-minute benefit?

Read zero and the business threshold separately

No. Both correspond to non-rejection of zero in their matching two-sided 5% tests, under the same assumptions and interval/test construction. But A lies entirely below the two-minute target: its uncertainty range supports only small changes. B still includes a worthwhile improvement of 2 minutes and worsening as large as 3 minutes. B leaves a substantially more consequential question unresolved.

Do not translate either result into “no effect.” Report the signed interval, the predeclared useful-effect threshold, and the study assumptions. For B, decide whether the value of resolving the uncertainty justifies further data. For A, a rollout justified only by a two-minute saving lacks support from this interval.

This comparison interprets uncertainty; it does not automatically perform an equivalence test. To establish that changes are acceptably small, predeclare both acceptable bounds and use an appropriate equivalence procedure with its own error control. Neither interval alone establishes causation or settles rollout costs.

14Separate evidence from business importanceA defensible conclusion, power, uncertainty and honest limits+

Return from the statistic to the operating decision

We have now chosen tests by outcome, design and target. Before recommending action, put the original business question back into the sentence. Does the evidence concern a delay, an improvement, a conversion gap or consistency? Then distinguish what the sample suggests from what the decision-maker is prepared to spend or risk.

“p=.03” is not a recommendation. A tiny effect can become statistically significant with enough observations, while an important effect can remain uncertain in a small sample. Decide what magnitude would matter before looking at the results.

Report what the data support

“The estimated change was 2 minutes. Under the independent normal model, the two-sided 5% test rejects the 30-minute benchmark; the matching 95% interval for the population mean is 30.04 to 33.96 minutes, or 0.04 to 3.96 minutes above the 30-minute benchmark. We should assess whether this difference changes staffing or the service promise.”

Do not overclaim

“The null has a 4.55% chance of being true,” “the alternative is proved,” or “no significant difference means the processes are equivalent.” A test does not validate its own sampling assumptions.

  • Report the target population, sampling unit, sample sizes, effect estimate, SE, test statistic, df, alternative, p-value, α and confidence interval.
  • Explain randomization or sampling, independence, pairing, distributional assumptions and approximation checks.
  • Disclose exclusions, missing-data handling, multiple comparisons and any deviation from the original analysis plan.
  • Do not repeatedly peek and stop at p<.05 without a sequential design. Testing many metrics also raises the chance of at least one false alarm; choose a suitable multiplicity strategy.
  • For non-rejection, discuss which practically important effects the interval still permits. Plan additional data using power at a meaningful alternative, not only the observed p-value.

Significance is evidence against a model at a chosen error threshold. Practical significance asks whether the size of the effect justifies action, considering costs, risks and uncertainty.

CHECK YOUR JUDGMENT

Can you defend
the decision?

Check the question, the model and the conclusion, not only the arithmetic.

Written practice and solutions
  1. A small p-value means H₀ is probably false? No. It measures tail probability under H₀, not the probability of H₀.
  2. Which tail after seeing a positive estimate? The tail chosen before seeing the data. Switching afterward invalidates the intended test.
  3. Rejecting a true null is which error? Type I, a false positive when rejection means positive.
  4. Can a non-rejection establish no meaningful effect? No. Inspect the interval and power; demonstrating equivalence needs a different design.
  5. Unknown σ, 25 independent normal observations? Use a one-sample t test with 24 df.
  6. A left-sided t critical value? It is negative: −t at lower-tail probability 1−α.
  7. Independent groups with no justified common variance? Welch’s test uses separate variance contributions and fractional df.
  8. Paired before and after data? Use one difference per pair, its SD and n−1 df. Include covariance through the difference.
  9. Equal-proportion null means equal observed fractions? No. It constrains the population probabilities; sample fractions may differ.
  10. Which SE for the basic two-proportion interval? Unpooled. The pooled equality test is not its exact inverse.
  11. Swap the groups in an F test? Reciprocate the statistic and swap numerator and denominator df. The two-sided p-value stays the same.
  12. Does statistical significance imply a useful improvement? No. Evaluate the size, interval, decision costs and study design.

Editorial corrections

Type I is a false positive under rejection-as-positive labeling. Non-rejection is not acceptance. Left-tail cutoffs have the correct negative sign. The pooled proof uses σ√(1/n₁+1/n₂). Welch’s standardized difference, not the variance sum, receives an approximate t reference, with fractional df retained.

Paired variance includes covariance, and normal marginals alone do not establish normal differences. Equal population proportions do not imply equal observed fractions or a uniformly smaller pooled SE. F reciprocals swap df; lower critical values are calculated rather than copying the source’s decimal error. Excel one-tail results are interpreted against the predeclared direction.
