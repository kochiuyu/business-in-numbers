STEP 01 OF 09

Name the number

{{rvvisual:mapping}}

Go deeper when you are ready. Full explanations and practice are below.

Talk it through · Separate the full outcome from the number you need.

1. Separate the full outcome from the number you need.

Two sales calls can produce no sales, a sale on just the first, a sale on just the second, or two sales. A staffing plan might care only about the count: 0, 1, 1, 2. Different outcomes can lead to the same number. That mapping is what “random variable” means; the variable itself is a rule, not a number chosen without a rule.

If the two calls are independent with purchase probability .5 each, their four outcomes are equally likely. Count 1 receives two outcomes and therefore probability .5; counts 0 and 2 each receive .25. The three possible counts are not equally likely merely because there are three of them.

01Turn an outcome into a useful numberRandom variables, support and the model you choose+

A random variable is a function from outcomes to numbers. For two sales calls, outcomes might be NN, NY, YN and YY. Define X as the number of sales: X(NN)=0, X(NY)=X(YN)=1 and X(YY)=2. The outcome records the order; X deliberately forgets it.

The support is the set of values the variable can take under the model. Here it is {0,1,2}, not the four-outcome sample space. A different business question could use a different variable, such as revenue or time to complete the calls.

Discrete: count or list

Finite or countably infinite possible values. Sales count, defect count, or prices restricted to a fixed set. Discrete values need not be integers: a payoff of $0, $2.50 or $7 is discrete.

Continuous: measure

We model fill volume or waiting time with a density over an interval. Recorded values may be rounded, but the underlying model can still be continuous.

Model ≠ reality

Choose the model for the decision and measurement precision. A stock quote moves in ticks; a continuous approximation may still be useful. It is an assumption, not a fact about every price.

Technical depth · What “continuous” means here

Our continuous examples are absolutely continuous: they have a density. An uncountable support alone does not guarantee a density; mixed and singular distributions also exist. A discrete support can be countably infinite, as with a Poisson count. A deterministic mapping can also collapse many outcomes into one number.

Class 1 averaged observed values. Here we average possible values using their probabilities as weights. For the 100-slot illustration, total orders are 0 × 10 + 1 × 30 + 2 × 40 + 3 × 20 = 170, or 1.7 per slot. Dividing all the counts by 100 gives the expectation formula below. The expected value need not itself be a possible outcome.

Spread works the same way: square each departure from 1.7, weight it by probability, and add. The result .81 has squared units; its square root .9 is in orders. Adding a fixed fee changes every revenue by the same amount, so it changes the mean but not the spread.

03Average outcome is not a guaranteed outcomeExpectation, variance, standard deviation and transformations+

Let x run through every possible value and p(x) give its probability. Multiply each value by its probability and add to obtain the mean μ. For variance, use the same probability weights on the squared distances from μ. The sigma symbol Σ means “add these terms,” not “multiply them.”

{{math:expected}}

For the shop, E[X]=0(.1)+1(.3)+2(.4)+3(.2)=1.7 orders. Variance is .81 orders², and SD is .9 orders. The long-run average can be 1.7 even though no slot contains a fractional order. A mean is neither the most likely value nor a service guarantee.

Revenue R=20X+5 adds a fixed $5 fee to $20 per order. Its expected value is $39, variance is 324 dollars², and SD is $18. Adding a constant moves the distribution without spreading it out; multiplying stretches the deviations.

4. Combining channels requires knowing how they move together.

A kiosk and app can each have purchase probability .5, yet the staffing burden depends on whether purchases occur together. If both always sell together, there are either zero or two purchases. If exactly one always sells, the total is always one. Both situations have expected total one, but very different variability.

Check that both marginal probabilities stay .5 and the expected total stays 1, while total variance changes. Covariance records the connection that individual means and variances miss.

TRY A SMALL COMPARISON · SEPARATE TEACHING EXAMPLE

A flat linear signal can hide a relationship

Predict first: If positive and negative movements cancel in covariance, does that mean knowing X tells you nothing about Y?

Y is determined by X

Let X be −1, 0 or 1, each with probability 1/3, and set Y = X². Then E[X] = 0, E[Y] = 2/3 and E[XY] = (−1 + 0 + 1)/3 = 0. So Cov(X,Y) = 0, yet P(Y=0 | X=0) = 1.

Same marginals, independent

Keep P(X=−1) = P(X=0) = P(X=1) = 1/3, with P(Y=0) = 1/3 and P(Y=1) = 2/3. Now multiply these marginal probabilities for every pair. Covariance is again 0, but P(Y=0 | X=0) = 1/3.

Interpret what changed

The separate distributions of X and Y are unchanged, as is their zero covariance. What changes is the joint pattern. In the first case, X determines Y exactly through a curve. In the second, learning X does not change Y’s probabilities. Covariance measures linear co-movement; zero covariance alone is not a test of independence.

Technical depth · Independence, a counterexample and the proof

For discrete variables, independence means p(x,y)=pX(x)pY(y) for every pair. It implies zero covariance when moments exist. The converse is false: let X be equally likely −1, 0 or 1 and Y=X². Cov(X,Y)=0, yet Y is determined by X. Correlation divides covariance by σXσY and is undefined if either SD is zero.

Choose a count model

{{rvvisual:counts}}

Go deeper when you are ready. Full explanations and practice are below.

Talk it through · Select a model from the process, not from the curve’s name.

5. Select a model from the process, not from the curve’s name.

Ask first what is fixed. One yes/no trial suggests Bernoulli. A fixed number of independent trials with a common chance suggests binomial. A count of arrivals during a fixed exposure at a stable independent-arrival rate suggests Poisson. Sampling distinct items from a known finite batch suggests hypergeometric because each selection changes what remains.

These are not interchangeable labels for “counts.” Twelve website visits with independent purchase chance .25 are binomial trials; twelve minutes of arriving orders may use a Poisson process model instead. A discrete uniform model requires equally likely values, not merely a finite list.

Pause and explain: Why does inspecting without replacement from a fixed batch differ from twelve independent visits?

Compare your reasoning

After an inspected item is removed, the remaining batch composition changes. The chance of the next tagged item depends on earlier selections. A binomial model assumes a common probability and independent trials; a known finite batch sampled uniformly without replacement instead gives a hypergeometric count.

05Count successes, arrivals, or a sample?Discrete uniform, Bernoulli, binomial, Poisson and hypergeometric+

Discrete uniform

Every value in a specified finite set is equally likely. Example: a dispatch system randomly assigns one of six numbered loading bays.

{{math:discrete-uniform}}

For consecutive values 1,…,m, mean=(m+1)/2 and variance=(m²−1)/12. Two fair coin tosses do not produce a uniform count of heads: 0,1,2 have probabilities 1/4,1/2,1/4.

Bernoulli: one binary trial

X=1 if a customer purchases, X=0 otherwise. “Success” names the counted outcome; it need not be desirable.

{{math:bernoulli}}

At p=0 or 1 there is no variation. Variance is largest at p=.5.

Binomial: fixed number of trials

Count purchases among 12 independent visits, each with the same purchase probability .25. The count has mean 3 and variance 2.25. Shared promotions, household visits or differing propensities may invalidate this model.

{{math:binomial}}

At p=0 or 1, use the corresponding point mass. Otherwise n is a nonnegative integer and 0<p<1.

Poisson: arrivals per exposure

Assume orders arrive independently at a stable rate of 4 per hour. An hour’s count has mean and variance 4; half an hour’s count has mean and variance 2. Specify the exposure before using λ.

{{math:poisson}}

The support is unbounded, even though a displayed plot is finite. A Poisson count can represent outages per week, not fractional hours of downtime. Clustering and changing rates often produce more variation than a simple Poisson allows.

Hypergeometric: without replacement

A batch has N=40 items, K=8 tagged for review. A simple random sample of n=6 distinct items is inspected. Count tagged items. Selection changes the composition remaining in the batch, so trials are dependent.

{{math:hypergeom}}{{math:hypergeom-moments}}

Here mean=1.2, variance≈.836923. The variance formula requires N>1. Here the finite-population factor is 34/39. Without replacement gives smaller count variance than independent replacement sampling with p=K/N. Sampling the whole batch makes the count exactly K, with variance zero. Population composition is fixed and known in this model; actual purchases or warranty claims are not automatically random samples.

Talk it through · See the logic behind a formula before memorizing it.

6. See the logic behind a formula before memorizing it.

Exactly two purchases in three independent visits can occur as SSN, SNS or NSS. If p = .25, each particular sequence has probability .25 × .25 × .75. The three sequences cannot happen simultaneously, so add them: 3 × .25² × .75 = .140625. The binomial coefficient counts those placements for a general number of visits.

That reasoning reuses Class 2’s multiplication rule along a sequence and addition across disjoint sequences. The optional proofs below extend this idea to moments, rare arrivals and sampling without replacement. You can proceed to density with that explanation even if you have not studied the algebra of limits.

Technical depth · Binomial arrangements and moments

A particular sequence with x successes and n−x failures has probability pˣ(1−p)ⁿ⁻ˣ by multiplying probabilities of independent intersections. There are C(n,x) disjoint such sequences. Sum over them.

{{math:binomial-proof}}

A shorter route writes X as the sum of n independent Bernoulli indicators with common p. Each has Xᵢ²=Xᵢ, giving mean p and variance p−p². Add the moments. Different success probabilities generally produce a different distribution.

Technical depth · Poisson as a rare-event limit

{{math:poisson-limit}}

The approximation is useful for large n and small p with λ=np, but is not an equality for a finite n. For n=100, p=.02, a Poisson model uses λ=2. Compare exact and approximate probabilities before trusting an operational tail.

{{math:poisson-proof}}

Technical depth · Hypergeometric moments and dependence

{{math:hypergeom-proof}}

Write X=I₁+⋯+Iₙ for the selected positions. Each indicator has mean K/N, while distinct positions satisfy E[IᵢIⱼ]=K(K−1)/(N(N−1)). The n(n−1) ordered pairs give the second factorial moment. Simplification produces the finite-population correction. Equivalently, shift the combinatorial sum using x C(K,x)=K C(K−1,x−1) and normalize the smaller hypergeometric distribution.

Talk it through · For continuous measurements, replace masses with areas.

7. For continuous measurements, replace masses with areas.

The histogram in Class 1 grouped measured times into intervals. A density model idealizes the distribution as a smooth or piecewise-flat curve. Its probability is area: height alone depends on units and can exceed one. A uniform wait over half an hour has density 2 per hour, but a quarter-hour interval has probability 2 × .25 = .5.

A continuous CDF still answers “at most,” just as before. Subtract F(a) from F(b) to keep only the area between a and b. A quantile asks the inverse question: instead of “how much probability by this deadline?”, ask “which deadline accumulates this probability?”

07A curve’s area answers the questionDensity, CDF, integration and quantiles+

In a density model, P(X=x)=0 at every single point. Probability belongs to intervals. The height f(x) is not the probability of exactly x. A density may exceed 1; the total area must equal 1. For a uniform wait from 0 to .5 hours, the density is 2 per hour and the area is 2×.5=1.

{{math:density-def}}

For these continuous models, including or excluding an endpoint makes no difference. F(b)−F(a) works for any combination of open and closed interval endpoints. Use 1−F(b) for a right tail.

{{math:quantile}}

A 95th-percentile completion time is a time that 95% of the modeled completions do not exceed. It is not the time when the density equals .95. More generally qₚ=inf{x:F(x)≥p}; discrete CDF jumps may prevent exact equality.

Technical depth · From thin strips to integrals

{{math:density-derivative}}

The probability in a thin strip shrinks with its width. A Riemann sum adds f(xᵢ)Δx over strips; its limit is the area integral. Do not treat an individual point as carrying a tiny positive mass. Where F is continuous on [a,b] and differentiable inside, the mean value theorem supplies a point c with F(b)−F(a)=f(c)(b−a).

{{math:continuous-moments}}

The same linearity, second-moment and covariance identities apply. The dx records the integration variable and small width; it is not decoration to discard.

Talk it through · Match a waiting-time model to how waiting happens.

8. Match a waiting-time model to how waiting happens.

A pickup promised uniformly within a fixed window has bounded support and equal probability for equal-length subintervals. An exponential wait models a different mechanism: arrivals from a stable-rate Poisson process, with no scheduled deadline and no memory of how long you have already waited. A shared mean does not make those mechanisms equivalent.

Keep units attached to the rate. At .1 arrivals per minute, the mean wait is 1/.1 = 10 minutes and the expected hourly count is .1 × 60 = 6. The Poisson variable counts arrivals in an interval; the exponential variable measures time until the next arrival.

This model assumes a constant arrival rate; an appointment timetable, changing demand or a queue in front of you can invalidate it.

Uniform: equal density over a bounded interval

Assume a pickup time is uniformly distributed from a=2 to b=8 minutes. The mean is 5 minutes, variance 3 minutes², and P(3≤X≤5)=2/6=1/3. “Uniform” means equal probability for intervals of equal length, not positive probability at individual points.

Talk it through · Standardization measures distance in SDs rather than mL.

9. Standardization measures distance in SDs rather than mL.

A 496 mL fill is 4 mL below a 500 mL mean. If SD is 4 mL, it is one SD below: subtract 500 and divide by 4 to get −1. Standardization lets one standard-normal table answer questions for any normal mean and positive SD. It changes units, not the underlying event.

For an upper threshold exceeded 5% of the time, first translate “upper 5%” into cumulative probability .95. The standard-normal 95th percentile is about 1.645; convert back with 500 + 4 × 1.645 ≈ 506.58 mL. A central 95% interval splits 5% between two tails and therefore uses a different cutoff, about 1.96.

09Use the bell curve carefullyStandardization, tails, quantiles and the 68–95–99.7 rule+

Suppose a stable filling process is modeled as X~N(500,4²) mL. The first parameter is the mean; the second is variance. Excel and SciPy instead ask for SD=4 in their scale argument. The support of a normal is all real numbers, so check whether impossible values have negligible probability for your application.

{{math:normal-pdf}}

Below 496 mL corresponds to Z<−1, giving .1587. Above 504 also gives .1587. Between 496 and 504 gives .6827. The 95th percentile is 500+4×1.644854≈506.5794 mL. It is a one-sided threshold, not the upper end of the central 95% interval.

Technical depth · Approximation is not identification

The central limit theorem makes suitably standardized sums approximately normal under conditions, such as independent identically distributed terms with finite nonzero variance. It does not make every finite sum exactly normal. For a binomial approximation, a common check is np and n(1−p) both at least 10; use a continuity correction such as P(X≤k)≈Φ((k+.5−np)/√(np(1−p))). Check accuracy in important tails. A large-mean Poisson also approaches a normal shape after standardization.

Real financial returns can have heavy tails and changing volatility. Normal prices may allow negative prices; the Black–Scholes model instead uses lognormal stock prices and normal log returns under its assumptions. A familiar bell shape is not evidence that a model is safe.

Talk it through · Treat the normal formula as a model with a mathematical foundation.

10. Treat the normal formula as a model with a mathematical foundation.

A density must have total area one. The constant involving π in the normal formula provides that normalization; it is not a parameter estimated separately for each filling process. The optional derivations below explain where it comes from and how symmetry relates to the curve.

For now, retain the practical boundary: normality is an assumption about an entire distribution, including its tails. A symmetric-looking plot or matching skewness and kurtosis is not proof. Later sampling theory explains when approximately normal behavior can emerge for averages, even when individual observations have a different shape.

Technical depth · The Gaussian integral

{{math:gaussian}}

The density e⁻ˣ²/√π has variance 1/2 and SD 1/√2. Scaling it yields the normal density. The normal CDF has no elementary antiderivative; numerical functions or tables evaluate its integral.

Technical depth · Radial symmetry plus independent coordinates

Suppose a positive continuous planar density is both rotationally symmetric and a product h(x)h(y). Symmetry makes h even. Compare (x,y) with (√(x²+y²),0), then define q(s)=h(√s)/h(0) for s≥0.

{{math:radial}}

Taking logs makes an additive continuous function, so log q(s)=cs. Integrability excludes c≥0. This argument identifies a centered Gaussian under these strong symmetry and independence assumptions; it is not a universal explanation for business data.

Technical depth · Moments and standardized shape

{{math:normal-moments}}{{math:normal-shape}}

The raw fourth central moment is 3σ⁴, not generally 3. Matching skewness and kurtosis does not prove normality. For Z, repeated integration by parts gives E[Z⁴]=3E[Z²]=3.

Talk it through · The uncertainty of a statistic has a distribution too.

11. The uncertainty of a statistic has a distribution too.

The earlier models describe individual outcomes, such as one fill or one hour’s orders. Later we will calculate quantities from samples: a sample mean, a sample variance or a ratio of variances. Because a different sample gives a different result, those statistics are also random variables.

Chi-square arises from sums of squared independent standard-normal quantities. Student t allows a normal numerator to be scaled by an independent estimated spread, giving heavier tails. F compares two independent variance scales. Learn those roles now; Class 4 and the inference lessons supply the sampling conditions and applications.

Chi-square: add squares

For integer ν, each independent standard-normal ingredient supplies one squared term. The result is nonnegative: this explains why squared normal noise leads to chi-square.

t: divide by uncertain scale

The random denominator can be small, producing more extreme values than a standard normal. Independence of Z and U matters. Small sample size alone is not the construction.

F: compare two scales

Divide each chi-square quantity by its own degrees of freedom. Numerator df comes first. Swapping the scales gives a reciprocal with df order n, m, not the same F distribution in general.

Interpret what changed

Read each stack from top to bottom as a recipe. The ingredients, scaling and independence determine the distribution. The full lesson preserves densities, finite-moment conditions and the extension to positive real degrees of freedom. Under normal sampling, estimating the sample mean leaves n−1 independent degrees of freedom for sample variance.

11Three distributions you will meet againChi-square, Student t and F: constructions and conditions+

These families will matter when we infer population properties from samples. Here, learn what random ingredients create them. Degrees of freedom control their shapes.

Chi-square: add squared normal noise

{{math:chi-square}}

Nonnegative and right-skewed, with mean ν and variance 2ν. More degrees of freedom reduce relative skewness. One squared standard normal is χ²₁. Under normal sampling, squared deviations lead to chi-square results for sample variance; later goodness-of-fit tests use it too.

Student t: normal divided by uncertain scale

{{math:student-t}}

Symmetric around zero with heavier tails than the standard normal. The median is zero for all ν>0. The mean is zero only for ν>1; variance is ν/(ν−2) only for ν>2. For ν≤1 the mean is undefined; for 1<ν≤2 variance is infinite. Excess kurtosis is 6/(ν−4) only for ν>4. As ν grows, it approaches N(0,1).

Normal-sample mean inference with an estimated SD leads to t, usually with n−1 degrees of freedom. “Small sample” alone is not the full justification.

F: ratio of two independent variance scales

{{math:f-family}}

Positive and usually right-skewed. It has two degrees-of-freedom parameters; their order matters. Ratios of normal-sample variances and ANOVA lead here. Mean is n/(n−2) for n>2. Also T²~F₁,ν when T~tν. Do not replace these distributions by a normal just because a calculator is available.

Γ(k)=(k−1)! for positive integers, and B(a,b)=Γ(a)Γ(b)/Γ(a+b). The construction above uses integer degrees of freedom; these density families extend to positive real degrees of freedom. The tool section includes executable plots comparing shapes and numerical CDF/quantile examples.
